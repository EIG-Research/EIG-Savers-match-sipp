## 2. Declines and partials: does the rationale answer my objection?

**The 80-percent-as-central question (focus question 4) — partially conceded, with a sharpened residual.** Is 80 percent defensible as the central case while the rail rungs stay open? My answer: *80 percent is a defensible W-2 payroll-default rate; it is not a defensible uniform rate.* The evidence places a payroll default with a 50–200 percent match between auto-IRA stay-in (~64–68 percent, no match) and Madrian–Shea's 86 percent (auto-enrollment under an employer match), so 80 percent sits where I would put the W-2 rung myself. But the brief's $19.09B rung applies 80 percent uniformly — including to the 3.09M self-employed on the auto-enrolled margin, who face no default at all, and (a construction quirk worth a footnote) it also overrides *observed* saver behavior in both directions, paying only 80 percent of observed participants. Repricing the uniform rung by rail — 80 percent W-2, 20 percent self-employed — gives **$17.90B**, about $1.19B below the brief's central case. On the spec's headline construction (observed savers keep their behavior), my recommended rail-specific central — **W-2 at 80 percent, self-employed at 20 percent — lands at $16.62B with 30.5M participants** (68.6 percent overall take-up). So: the objection to a uniform point estimate stands, but its fiscal bite has shrunk to roughly a billion dollars, and the band is published. I treat this as a condition for Tier 2.1, not grounds to oppose.

**My recommended Tier 2.1 / 2.13 values (the changelog's direct question):**

- **W-2 payroll-default rate: 80 percent central, band 64–86.** Floor: state auto-IRA stay-in (CalSavers ~64 percent after opt-out; no match). Ceiling: Madrian–Shea 86 percent. The federal match — 50 percent minimum, up to 200 percent at the bottom — should compress opt-out below the no-match auto-IRA experience, justifying the upper half of that band. The brief's 80 percent is, conveniently, exactly my W-2 central; it just needs to be labeled as the *W-2 rail* assumption.
- **Self-employed rate: 20 percent central, band 14–30.** Duflo et al.'s 14 percent (a 50 percent match, presented face-to-face by a tax professional) is the floor — the best observed outcome for assisted opt-in. The D10 rails justify going above it: an estimated-tax election with auto-debit converts a repeated active choice into a one-time election with automatic execution — a quasi-default — and the match at the bottom of this band is far more generous than 50 percent. 30 percent is the optimistic case if platform-facilitated contribution defaults materialize at scale. Nothing here is a true payroll default, so the self-employed rung must stay well below the W-2 rung. The fiscal stakes are modest — the self-employed margin's full-participation cost is only $1.48B of the $16.01B margin — but the *coverage* claim for 4.26M self-employed eligibles rests on this rung, and 20 percent implies roughly 0.6–0.9M of them actually participate. Say so.

The refreshed closed form, for the record: **Cost(p) = $4.71B + $16.01B × p** on the 29.25M-worker auto-enrolled margin — $160M per percentage point of take-up — reproducing the $14.15B headline at p = 0.590 to the dollar. My literature band on the new base runs **$9.77B** (OregonSaves-effective 34.3 percent / Saver's-Credit 5.7 percent) **to $17.64B** (86 / 30).

## 3. Fresh eyes

**Tier 2.2 persistence anchor: 0.59 is the wrong anchor — recommend 0.80 central, 0.70–0.90 sensitivity.** The state auto-IRA persistence figure conflates two margins: active disenrollment and *mechanical churn* — accounts stranded at job change because the account is keyed to the employer relationship (OregonSaves' gap between ~64 percent enrollment and 34 percent positive balances is mostly this, in a segment with ~38 percent annual turnover). D2's worker-level portable account is engineered to delete the mechanical component, and the match raises the value of staying in. Using 0.59 would assume the design's signature feature fails completely; I would show 0.59 only as a "state-architecture counterfactual" the federal account is built to beat. At 0.75 persistence applied to the margin, my rail-specific central falls from $16.62B to $13.64B — persistence, not initial take-up, remains the largest behavioral unknown, and it is the parameter the S2 evaluation should be designed to measure.

**Tier 2.3 six-month withdrawal propensity: bound at 10 percent, central 5 percent.** Forfeiting a 50–200 percent match is a severe penalty on withdrawing inside the window, and own-contribution Roth liquidity means emergencies hit unmatched dollars first only if the worker understands the rule (see below). The leakage literature's alarming numbers accumulate over years, not six months. At 5 percent, the cost overstatement is ~$0.8B on my central case; the 10 percent bound is ~$1.7B. Material for honesty, immaterial for the verdict.

**The five focus questions, briefly.** (1) *The 200 percent floor:* defensible. My refreshed decomposition says the schedule above a flat 50 percent costs **$1.35B — 10 percent of the headline** (access is 53 percent; inframarginal transfer 37 percent) — so the floor is cheap, and "$500 unlocks $1,000" is the most legible sentence in the program. The D3 rationale is honest that the model cannot adjudicate a rate response; in the opt-in world Duflo et al. *did* find take-up rising in the match rate, so the bet is not evidence-free. Legislating the S2 randomized 50/100/200 arm is the right resolution and should be a statutory condition. (2) *Federal account:* keep it. Portability is the only feature on the field that attacks the enrollment-to-balance gap, which is my largest residual worry; the EO-marketplace alternative is an information remedy, and the information-only literature is brutal. Worth the political cost. (3) *Anchor population:* outside my core lane, but behaviorally the "two-thirds of the IRS single-filer median" rule is index-card legible and the ⅔ calibration reproduces coverage within ~3–4 percent; the decennial re-anchor's saw-tooth is tolerable because the prior-year basis means no worker's rate changes retroactively. (4) Answered in §2. (5) *Debt financing:* I defer to the fiscal panelists, with one behavioral note — the RAND-style safety-net offset operates through *accumulated balances*, so it inherits the persistence (2.2) and leakage (2.3) parameters; the offset paragraph should carry those caveats explicitly.

**One genuinely new concern: forfeiture legibility at the withdrawal screen.** D6 replaced the three-year recapture with a six-month match forfeiture — simpler, good. But the forfeiture is now a *hidden price on a liquidity event*: a present-biased worker tapping Roth-accessible contributions in month four forfeits up to twice the withdrawn amount in match, and nothing in the design makes that visible at the moment of withdrawal. Extend the D7 legibility layer to the withdrawal interface — "withdrawing $500 today forfeits $1,000 of match; in 8 weeks it forfeits nothing" — and to the D7 new-entrant edge case, default first-year workers with no prior return to the *most generous* band pending the first filing: never make a worker's first interaction with the program a clawback.
