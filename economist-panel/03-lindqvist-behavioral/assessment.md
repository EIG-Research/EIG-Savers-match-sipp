# Assessment: Universal Account, Targeted Saver's Match — Behavioral Economics Review

**Panelist 03 — Dr. Owen Lindqvist, behavioral economist (defaults and choice architecture).**
**Date: 2026-06-10.**

I work in the tradition of Madrian–Shea and Beshears–Choi–Laibson–Madrian: my priors are that enrollment mechanics, defaults, and salience — not subsidy rates — determine who participates in retirement saving, and that subsidy generosity mostly determines how much money flows to people who would have saved anyway. I therefore read this proposal not as a match redesign with an account attached, but as an auto-enrollment program with a match attached, and I evaluated it accordingly: I recomputed the cost-versus-take-up curve in closed form from the repository's row-level simulation frame, decomposed the headline cost into the price of access versus the price of generosity, and stress-tested the headline behavioral assumption against the experimental and quasi-experimental participation literature.

All computations below run from `code/01_takeup_sensitivity_curve.R` and `code/02_default_vs_match_decomposition.R` against `data/processed/universal_sm_hybrid/simulation_results.parquet`; outputs are in `tables/` and `figures/`.

## 1. The cost-take-up curve: the headline sits inside the literature's band, but the band is wide

The headline construction is exactly linear in the participation rate. Workers with an observed defined-contribution status (15.99 million weighted) keep their observed behavior, contributing a fixed $5.01 billion; the 30.07 million newly auto-enrolled workers (the behavioral margin) enter at rate *p*, contributing *p* × $16.62 billion. Cost(*p*) = $5.01B + $16.62B × *p*, which reproduces the repo headline to the dollar at *p* = 0.598 ($14.95 billion) and implies a marginal cost of roughly **$166 million per percentage point of take-up** on the auto-enrolled margin. The uniform alternative is Cost(*p*) = $24.92B × *p*. The full curves are in `tables/takeup_cost_curve.csv` and **`figures/fig1_takeup_sensitivity_curve.png`**.

Overlaying verified literature benchmarks on the headline-construction curve (`tables/literature_markers.csv`):

| Behavioral benchmark | Take-up | Annual cost |
|---|---|---|
| Madrian–Shea 401(k) auto-enrollment, new hires under a 50 percent employer match | 86 percent | $19.3 billion |
| CalSavers auto-IRA stay-in rate (35.9 percent opt out, July 2024) | 64 percent | $15.7 billion |
| OregonSaves: share of eligible workers with positive balances, April 2020 | 34.3 percent | $10.7 billion |
| Duflo et al. H&R Block experiment, 50 percent salient match, opt-in | 14 percent | $7.3 billion |
| Saver's Credit claiming, share of all returns, 2021 | 5.7 percent | $6.0 billion |

Three lessons. First, the proposal's $14.9 billion headline is bracketed by the auto-IRA experience ($15.7 billion at CalSavers' stay-in rate) and is not an outlier — the 59.8 percent assumption sits between initial auto-IRA enrollment (~64–68 percent after opt-out; OregonSaves average monthly opt-out 32.5 percent, CalSavers 31.5–35.9 percent) and OregonSaves' sobering *effective* participation of 34.3 percent with positive balances after job turnover and attrition (Chalmers, Mitchell, Reuter, and Zhong, "Auto-Enrollment Retirement Plans for the People: Choices and Outcomes in OregonSaves," NBER Working Paper 28469, 2021, https://www.nber.org/papers/w28469.). Second, the entire *plausible* behavioral range — from auto-IRA-effective to Madrian–Shea — spans $10.7 to $19.3 billion, roughly −28 percent to +29 percent around the headline. Fiscal scoring of this proposal is a behavioral-assumptions exercise before it is anything else, and the closed-form linearity means Congress can be shown the whole curve rather than a point estimate. Third, the opt-in benchmarks ($6.0–$7.3 billion) are not alternative cost scenarios — they are what this program costs *if you delete the default*, which is precisely the §6433/TrumpIRA.gov status quo. The distance between $7.3 billion and $14.9 billion is the fiscal footprint of auto-enrollment doing its job.

## 2. Which lever does the work? Access is six times the price of generosity

The proposal has two levers: 3 percent auto-enrollment (access) and the 200-percent-floor MAGI-linear match (generosity). I re-priced every eligible worker's match under a flat 50 percent rate — `min(0.5 × default_contrib, $1,000)`, identical eligibility band and cap — and decomposed the headline (`tables/decomposition_table.csv`, **`figures/fig2_access_vs_generosity.png`**):

| Component | Cost | Share of headline |
|---|---|---|
| Flat 50 percent match to observed savers (no behavioral change) | $5.70B | 38 percent |
| **Cost of access**: auto-enrolled margin at flat 50 percent | $7.95B | 53 percent |
| **Cost of generosity**: match rate above 50 percent (200-percent-floor schedule) | $1.29B | 9 percent |
| Headline total | $14.95B | 100 percent |

The result is stark and, to a defaults person, deeply satisfying: **the match-rate redesign — the proposal's most eye-catching feature — accounts for only 9 percent of the headline cost.** Two mechanics compress it. The $1,000 cap binds quickly at high rates, so doubling or quadrupling the rate on a 3 percent contribution of low earnings adds modest dollars. And the linear schedule claws back as well as it gives: on the employer-plan route the "generosity" component is *negative* ($−0.40 billion; `tables/decomposition_by_route.csv`) because workers between the pivot and the endpoint face rates *below* 50 percent, while the universal-account route gains $1.69 billion. The 200 percent floor is nearly self-financing within the band. Meanwhile 53 percent of the money buys new savers (access) and 38 percent is an inframarginal transfer to people already observed saving — the Duflo–Saez-style windfall share that any match program carries.

The policy implication: defenders of this proposal should spend no political capital defending the 200 percent number, which is cheap, and all of it defending auto-enrollment, which is the program. Conversely, critics who attack the "200 percent match" as profligate are attacking $1.3 billion of a $14.9 billion program.

## 3. The headline behavioral assumption: right on average, wrong by rail

The headline applies the SIPP-observed conditional DC participation rate — 59.8 percent among *eligible* workers with an existing account (note: 71.6 percent in the full universe; the eligible-band rate is lower because participation rises with income; `tables/behavioral_parameters.csv`) — uniformly to all 30.07 million newly auto-enrolled workers. That margin is heterogeneous: 26.89 million W-2 workers with a payroll rail ($15.08 billion full-participation cost) and 3.18 million self-employed workers with none ($1.54 billion).

**For W-2 workers, 59.8 percent is likely too low.** The observed 59.8 percent is *opt-in* behavior; the proposal imposes opt-out. Madrian and Shea, "The Power of Suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) Participation and Savings Behavior," QJE, 2001, https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682, found participation among new hires rose to roughly 86 percent under automatic enrollment versus 37–49 percent under opt-in — under a *less* generous match than this proposal offers the bottom of the band. The auto-IRA programs, the closest institutional analog to the federal universal account (mandated payroll deduction, no employer match, low-income population), show initial stay-in around 64–68 percent (ASPPA, "How Are Employees Responding to State-Run Retirement Plans?," 2024, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2024/8/how-are-employees-responding-state-run-retirement-plans/.) — and unlike auto-IRAs, this program adds a federal match of 50 to 200 percent, which should reduce opt-out further. The honest caveat runs the other way: OregonSaves' *effective* participation (positive balances) was only 34.3 percent, dragged down by 38.2 percent annual job turnover in this labor market segment. Stickiness across job transitions — not initial enrollment — is where the universal account must beat the state programs, and its federal portability is designed to do exactly that.

**For the self-employed, 59.8 percent is indefensible.** There is no payroll to deduct from; participation is an active, repeated choice. The relevant benchmark is Duflo, Gale, Liebman, Orszag, and Saez, "Saving Incentives for Low- and Middle-Income Families: Evidence from a Field Experiment with H&R Block," QJE, 2006, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11680: a 50 percent match, presented face-to-face at tax time with professional assistance, achieved **14 percent** take-up. Repricing only the self-employed margin at 14 percent trims $0.70 billion (4.7 percent of headline) — small in dollars, but the proposal's coverage claims for 4.41 million self-employed eligibles rest on a rail that does not exist. My rail-specific repricing (`tables/literature_scenarios.csv`): W-2 at 86 percent and self-employed at 14 percent gives **$18.2 billion** (33.1 million participants); W-2 at OregonSaves-effective 34.3 percent and self-employed at Saver's-Credit-like 5.7 percent gives **$10.3 billion** (19.0 million). The headline is comfortably inside this band but should be presented as a band.

**Contribution-side salience.** The MAGI-linear schedule means no worker can state their own match rate: it varies continuously with a tax aggregate they learn after year-end (a worker's rate moves about 4.6 percentage points per $1,000 of Single MAGI). The comprehension literature is unkind to subtle match structures: Choi, Laibson, and Madrian, "$100 Bills on the Sidewalk: Suboptimal Investment in 401(k) Plans," NBER Working Paper 11554, 2005, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11554, found 20 to 60 percent of older workers forgo *arbitrage-free* employer match money — up to 6 percent of annual pay — and that an information treatment did not fix it. Duflo et al. likewise found the Saver's Credit, an equivalent-value but opaque incentive, produced "much more modest effects" than the transparent H&R Block match. RSAA's contribution-rate-keyed 100/50 schedule (S.1526, 119th Congress) is payroll-legible — "the government doubles your first 3 percent" fits on an index card; "your rate is 200 minus 4.56 per thousand of MAGI percent" does not. Two saving graces: the model conservatively assumes everyone stays at the 3 percent default (no opt-up is priced in), and under a default architecture comprehension governs *opt-up to the cap*, not participation. But the proposal's own diagnostic concedes the 3 percent default reaches the $1,000 cap only near the middle of the MFJ band — the remaining match dollars are $100 bills the literature says will stay on the sidewalk. The match also arrives as a tax-time deposit a year after the contribution, the weakest possible reinforcement schedule for a present-biased saver.

## 4. Political economy: §6433's missing rail, the EO as information remedy, and RSAA

The Saver's Match passed because it was the rare redistributive provision with a bipartisan pedigree — Cardin–Portman lineage, folded into SECURE 2.0 (P.L. 117-328, Division T) on a 68–29 Senate omnibus vote — and because converting the nonrefundable Saver's Credit into a refundable deposit was an unambiguous technical improvement. The credit it replaces is the cleanest natural experiment in opt-in design failure we have: 5.7 percent of taxpayers claimed it in 2021, average $191, and approximately **0 percent** of filers with AGI below $10,000 claimed in 2022 (CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). Refundability fixes the liability problem; §6433 still leaves the behavioral problem untouched. The match requires an existing account and an affirmative contribution — and as of June 2026 there are no proposed regulations, plans are not required to accept match deposits, and IRSAC warns the program's success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions" (ASPPA, 2026). §6433 built a 50 percent match and forgot to build the enrollment mechanism. Duflo et al.'s 14 percent is my central forecast for what an opt-in 50 percent match achieves under ideal salience; Saver's Credit history says realized claiming among the bottom of the eligible band will be worse.

The April 30, 2026 executive order and TrumpIRA.gov are, from my chair, a pure information-and-search-cost remedy: a comparison portal listing low-fee, no-minimum IRAs that accept match deposits, with no account creation, no default, no auto-enrollment, and no new money (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026.). The literature's verdict on information-only interventions is consistent and brutal: the Choi–Laibson–Madrian information treatment moved nothing even when the money was free; Duflo et al. needed a seated tax professional plus a transparent match to reach 14 percent. Lowering fees from search frictions is genuinely useful — the 0.15 percent cap is good consumer protection, and only about 6.4 percent of target-date funds clear it (Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.) — but my point prediction for the portal's standalone effect on match take-up among the 41 million targeted workers is low single digits. The EO's own Section 6, directing legislative recommendations for codification with "automatic portfolio choices," implicitly concedes the diagnosis: the binding constraint is the default, and defaults require statute. The proposal under review supplies exactly the two things the EO cannot: an account that exists by default and a contribution that happens by default.

Against RSAA (S.1526), the architectures are siblings — universal federal account, 3 percent auto-enrollment, refundable match deposited to the account. The behavioral trade is the match key: RSAA's contribution-rate schedule is more legible and gives a marginal incentive to opt up (each payroll dollar up to 5 percent is visibly matched), while the hybrid's MAGI key is better targeted but invisible at the paycheck. My decomposition says this trade is worth less than the rhetoric suggests — the rate geometry is only 9 percent of cost — but legibility compounds over decades of opt-up decisions. RSAA's 1 percent unconditional federal contribution is also behaviorally meaningful for exactly the population whose participation elasticity is lowest. On the other side, Morningstar's simulation warning that RSAA's 3 percent default could crowd down future wealth for workers who would otherwise land in higher-default 401(k)s (Look and VanDerhei, Morningstar, 2024.) applies with equal force here: a 3 percent default is also an anchor, and the proposal inherits that risk on its employer-plan route.

## Verdict: **Support with modifications**

The proposal correctly identifies that §6433's flaw is architectural, not parametric, and it spends its money accordingly — 53 percent on access, 9 percent on rate generosity. That is the right ratio, and no competing instrument on the field (the EO, the unimplemented §6433 status quo) supplies the default that does the work.

**Recommendations, in priority order:**

1. **Replace the uniform 59.8 percent take-up with rail-specific assumptions and publish the band, not the point.** W-2 payroll auto-enrollment belongs at 64–86 percent (auto-IRA stay-in to Madrian–Shea); self-employed belongs at 14 percent or below until a contribution rail exists. My computed band is $10.3–$19.3 billion (`tables/literature_scenarios.csv`); the closed-form curve ($5.01B + $166M per take-up point) makes this trivial to communicate. Build attrition-at-job-change into the model — OregonSaves' gap between 64 percent enrollment and 34 percent positive balances is the single most relevant cautionary statistic for the universal account.
2. **Engineer the self-employed rail or stop counting those workers in headline coverage.** Default the match into estimated-tax filings (a Schedule SE checkbox with auto-debit), or route through 1099-payer withholding. Duflo et al. is the ceiling for unassisted opt-in; 4.41 million eligibles deserve better than a rail that does not exist.
3. **Keep the MAGI-linear schedule but make it legible at the point of contribution.** Since the rate redesign costs only $1.3 billion net, keep the targeting — but require the account statement and payroll interface to display a personalized dollar figure ("contribute $62/month and the government adds up to $1,000/year"), add default auto-escalation toward the cap-reaching contribution within the eligibility band, and deposit the match with the shortest feasible lag. The schedule can be progressive in MAGI as long as the worker-facing object is a simple dollar promise; otherwise Choi–Laibson–Madrian tells us the cap-reaching match dollars will go unclaimed.
