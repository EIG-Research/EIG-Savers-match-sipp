# Political-Economy Assessment: The Universal-Account / Saver's Match Hybrid

**Panelist 08 — Dr. Grace Liu, political economy of retirement policy**
**Date: 2026-06-11**

I evaluate policy proposals the way they are actually decided: as coalition problems, not optimization problems. My questions are who is organized, who holds the veto points, what the score says, and what the last three attempts at this idea teach about the next one. By those tests the hybrid proposal under review is substantively the strongest universal-account design yet drafted — and politically passable only after one major amputation.

## 1. The beneficiary coalition: large, bottom-heavy, and mostly unorganized

My constituency mapping (`code/01_constituency_map.R`, from `data/processed/universal_sm_hybrid/simulation_results.parquet`; full table at `tables/constituency_by_filing_route.csv`, plotted in `figures/fig1_constituency_map.png`) decomposes the 46.07 million eligible workers and $24.9 billion in full-participation match dollars:

| Filing group | Route | Eligible (M) | Share of eligible | Match $ (B, full part.) | Share of dollars | Mean match rate (pp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single / MFS | Employer plan | 9.31 | 20.2% | $4.27 | 17.2% | 66.7 |
| Single / MFS | Federal universal account | 14.13 | 30.7% | $6.88 | 27.6% | 84.5 |
| Head of household | Employer plan | 3.60 | 7.8% | $2.37 | 9.5% | 70.9 |
| Head of household | Federal universal account | 3.80 | 8.2% | $2.73 | 11.0% | 102.4 |
| Married filing jointly | Employer plan | 6.50 | 14.1% | $3.81 | 15.3% | 60.0 |
| Married filing jointly | Federal universal account | 8.73 | 19.0% | $4.85 | 19.5% | 77.7 |
| **All** | **All routes** | **46.07** | **100%** | **$24.92** | **100%** | **76.6** |

Three political readings. First, the modal beneficiary is a single filer (23.44 million, 50.9 percent of eligibles, 44.8 percent of dollars) — low-income, low-turnout, residentially dispersed, and represented by no membership organization. In Arnold's terms this is a textbook inattentive public: real benefits, weak traceability, no lobby. Second, the politically valuable bloc is the 15.23 million married-filing-jointly eligibles (33.1 percent of eligibles, $8.66 billion), who remain eligible up to $87,678 of joint MAGI — squarely the dual-earner suburban households both parties now contest. A smart legislative sponsor leads with them, not with the bottom decile. Third, and most consequential: the routing split. The federal universal account carries 26.65 million workers (57.9 percent of eligibles) and $14.47 billion (58.1 percent of dollars), including all 4.41 million eligible self-employed workers; the employer-plan route carries 19.41 million workers already served by recordkeepers. The majority of the program's beneficiaries therefore sit in the one channel that has no incumbent institutional champion — and one well-organized institutional opponent, the firms that would rather serve those 26.65 million accounts themselves.

## 2. The losers' map is empty — among beneficiaries

Concentrated losers kill bills more reliably than diffuse winners pass them. I therefore computed the losers' map the charge requested (`tables/losers_map.csv`; `figures/fig3_match_rate_comparison.png`): within the current-law §6433 eligibility bands (TY2027: Single $20,500–$35,500; HoH $30,750–$53,250; MFJ $41,000–$71,000, per IRS Notice 2024-65), 4.01 million weighted workers (2.34M Single, 1.22M MFJ, 0.45M HoH) of the 33.08 million currently eligible fall in the region where the hybrid rate is below the 50 percent statutory headline. But comparing schedules point-by-point, even these workers come out ahead: their mean hybrid match rate is 44.4 percent against a mean current-law phased rate of 4.6 percent, because the hybrid's endpoints ($43,839 / $65,758 / $87,678) sit far above current law's upper thresholds. The weighted count of strict rate losers is **zero**, and at an identical $2,000 contribution the weighted count of strict dollar losers is also **zero**. No worker eligible under the enacted Saver's Match would see a reduced match under the hybrid. That is a rare and politically precious property — the redesign is a Pareto improvement across the existing beneficiary file, so the "they are cutting your match" attack has no factual basis.

The real concentrated losers are upstream. The federal account diverts a 26.65-million-customer market from IRA providers and recordkeepers; that, not beneficiary arithmetic, is where the opposition will form — exactly as it formed against the RSAA.

## 3. Why §6433 passed, and what the timeline teaches

The timeline (`figures/fig2_policy_timeline.png`, `code/02_timeline.R`) shows both enactments — the 2001 Saver's Credit and the 2022 Saver's Match — clearing as small components of giant bipartisan vehicles (EGTRRA; Division T of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023), the second descending from the decade-long Cardin–Portman partnership through the unanimously reported EARN Act (S. Rept. 117-142) (CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). The fiscal fingerprint matters most: JCT scored §103 at just **−$9.318 billion over FY2023–2032, with zero revenue effect before FY2028** and annual costs of $1.7–2.1 billion thereafter (JCT, JCX-21-22, 2022, in repo at `Infrastructure/references/literature/papers/x-21-22_jct_jcx-21-22.pdf`; line extracted via `code/_jct_lookup.R`.). The delayed effective date pushed half the budget window's cost outside it, and JCT's low implied take-up — itself a consequence of the access gap — kept the rest small. §6433 passed, in other words, partly *because* it was expected not to work very well.

The hybrid inverts that bargain. Its auto-enrollment architecture exists precisely to raise take-up, so its headline cost ($14.9 billion per year at 59.8 percent take-up; $24.9 billion ceiling) is roughly seven times JCT's annual §103 outlay. The lesson from 2022 is not "this can ride an omnibus unnoticed" — at this score it cannot — but rather: attach to a must-pass bipartisan retirement vehicle (a "SECURE 3.0"), keep a delayed effective date, and accept that the score is now the central battlefield. No JCT, CBO, or comparable external score of the hybrid exists; the repo's SIPP estimate has no official anchor, which is the same scoring vacuum that has helped stall the RSAA (panel policy-context brief, section C).

## 4. Why the RSAA stalled

The RSAA has been introduced three times (S.5271, 117th; S.3102/H.R.6065, 118th; S.1526/H.R.2696, 119th) with the same bipartisan quartet and has never received a hearing or markup; S.1526 sits in Senate Finance (Congress.gov, "S.1526 — Retirement Savings for Americans Act of 2025," 2025, https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1526.). Its endorsement coalition — AARP, Charles Schwab, EIG, Chamber of Progress (Hickenlooper press release, May 1, 2025, `_shared/sources/hickenlooper-rsaa-reintroduction-press-2025.html`.) — is broad but shallow at the committee level, while the opposition is narrow and deep: the American Retirement Association argues a subsidized federal account would induce small employers to drop 401(k)s (ASPPA, "Federally Run Retirement Plan Bill Reintroduced," 2025, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2025/5/federally-run-retirement-plan-bill-reintroduced/.), Morningstar's simulations gave opponents an empirical text — projected retirement wealth roughly 20 percent lower for Gen Z under baseline crowd-out assumptions (Morningstar, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.) — and there is still no official score of a program whose federal contribution scales to 5 percent of payroll. Diffuse-winner, concentrated-loser, unscored, gatekept: the RSAA is a four-for-four case study in why access bills die in Finance. Its original Senate champions on the enacted match, Cardin and Portman, have meanwhile both left the Senate (background), so the committee entrepreneurship that carried §6433 has no obvious heir.

## 5. What the April 2026 EO reveals — and the window it opens

The executive order (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," April 30, 2026, `_shared/sources/trumpira-eo-2026-04-30-whitehouse.html`.) is revealed preference in three acts. First, *market-listing over public option*: confronted with the same access gap the RSAA targets, the administration chose a Treasury comparison portal of private IRAs — sub-0.15 percent fees, no minimums, §6433(e)(2)(C) match acceptance — not a federal account. The contrast with the RSAA's TSP-like federal program is deliberate, and the insurance industry praised exactly that choice ("without creating a new government-run program," IRI, in 401(k) Specialist, "What They're Saying: Industry Reaction to Trump Retirement Plan Executive Order," May 1, 2026, `_shared/sources/401kspecialist-eo-industry-reaction-2026.html`.). Second, *branding politics*: naming the portal TrumpIRA.gov converts a §104(a) promotion duty into a personalized asset, which raises the political cost for this administration of any Democratic-coded alternative architecture — but also signals that retirement access is now an issue the right wants to own, a genuine novelty. Third, *the codification ask*: Section 6 directs Treasury and the NEC to draft legislative recommendations to codify low fees, match eligibility, diversified defaults, and portability. An EO that asks Congress to legislate is an EO conceding it cannot finish the job: no accounts, no auto-enrollment, no new money (panel policy-context brief, section B).

The window this opens is real but shaped. The industry consensus that formed around the EO — including ARA's Graff: "We strongly support automatic enrollment of uncovered workers and expansion of the Saver's Match" (401(k) Specialist EO roundup, 2026.) — now endorses two-thirds of the hybrid: auto-enrollment and a bigger match. Even Teresa Ghilarducci, whose 2021 work with Kevin Hassett — now the administration's chief economic adviser — is the intellectual ancestor of this design family, called the EO's underlying move "exactly right" while insisting Congress must finish it (same roundup.). What the window is *not* open for is the federal universal account itself: ICI's post-State of the Union line — build on the private system "rather than shifting to a government-run one-size-fits-all approach" (401(k) Specialist, "Groups Respond to Trump Retirement Plan Proposal," February 2026, `sources/401kspecialist-groups-respond-sotu-proposal-2026.html`.) — plus the MyRA precedent (30,000 participants; closed by Trump in 2017 as too costly, same source.) tells you how a Republican Congress will hear "new federal account administrator." My inference: the EO simultaneously opens the match-redesign-plus-auto-enrollment window and narrows the federal-account window, unless the account is rebranded as an extension of the administration's own portal — a possibility I flag as speculation, not prediction.

**Vulnerabilities.** (i) The federal account invites the "government takeover of your 401(k)" attack — the precise line that has held the RSAA in committee for three Congresses. (ii) The 200 percent floor invites "paying people to save other people's tax dollars": a 2:1 match has no precedent in the employer system, and a worker contributing $500 to draw $1,000 is one Finance Committee chart away from caricature, however sound the targeting logic. (iii) Median-indexing of pivots reads to budget hawks as a permanently auto-growing entitlement with no fixed nominal anchor — contrast §6433's CPI indexing. (iv) The auto-enrollment obligation activates NFIB's tested mandate playbook, even though the obligation is lighter than ERISA sponsorship.

**Assets.** (i) Forty-six million beneficiaries with zero losers among current-law eligibles (Section 2) — the defensive flank is fully covered, which was not true of the RSAA (whose 3 percent default could *reduce* projected wealth for future 401(k) participants per Morningstar). (ii) The $1,000 per-individual cap mirrors enacted §6433/Form 8880 structure: this is parameter reform of an existing bipartisan program, not a new program — a materially easier lift. (iii) Employer-mandate-lite: employers route payroll, they do not sponsor, match, or carry fiduciary duty; the state auto-IRA substitution clause gives the 12 operating state programs ($3.2 billion in assets by May 2026; Georgetown CRI, "State Program Performance Data — Historical Trends," 2026, https://cri.georgetown.edu/states/state-data/historical-trends/.) a stake in passage rather than preemption. (iv) The 15.23 million MFJ-couple bloc up to ~$88,000 gives swing-district members a middle-class constituency to claim.

## 7. Verdict: Support with modifications

A feasible 119th–120th Congress coalition exists for roughly 80 percent of this proposal: the match-rate redesign (with its empty losers' map), the per-individual cap, auto-enrollment routed through employer plans, state auto-IRAs, and EO-vetted private IRAs, attached to a Section 6 codification vehicle with a delayed effective date. The federal universal account, as drafted, is the element with three documented institutional opponents (ICI, ARA, IRI), one failed federal precedent (MyRA), and no committee champion — it is what must be traded away, or at minimum made a fallback rather than a default.

**Top three recommendations:**

1. **Swap the default channel.** Make the EO marketplace plus state auto-IRAs the default destination for unenrolled workers, with the federal TSP-like account as a backstop only where no qualifying private or state option exists (or as a competitively auctioned default provider). This converts ICI, recordkeepers, and fintech from opponents into bidders, and NAGDCA into an ally, at modest cost to the design's economics.
2. **Re-anchor the indexing and soften the floor's optics.** Index pivots to CPI from a statutory base (re-anchoring to SIPP medians only by future act of Congress), and present the schedule as "up to a $1,000 federal match, reachable on a $500 contribution for the lowest earners" rather than "a 200 percent match rate." Same policy, survivable framing; it also blunts the permanent-entitlement attack.
3. **Buy the score down with the §6433 playbook.** Seek a JCT score early, set the effective date two tax years out, and stage the auto-enrollment mandate by employer size (mirroring SECURE 2.0 §101's small-employer carve-outs) to keep the ten-year number nearer $100 billion than $150 billion and to defuse NFIB at the committee stage — where, per Pew, its own members are softer than its lobbyists.
