# Panel Assessment — Marcus Hale, Public Finance

**Reviewer:** Dr. Marcus Hale (conservative public-finance economist, Chicago price-theory tradition)
**Date:** 2026-06-10
**Subject:** "Universal Account, Targeted Saver's Match" hybrid proposal (`Infrastructure/specs/2026-05-28_universal-account-savers-match-hybrid-proposal.md`)
**Verdict:** **Oppose as designed** (support a stripped-down access-only variant)

## Vantage point

I evaluate retirement subsidies the way I evaluate any federal spending program: by the marginal dollar, not the average one. The relevant questions are how much of the outlay is inframarginal — flowing to behavior that would have occurred anyway — how much private saving the public subsidy crowds out, and what distortions the financing and the phaseout geometry introduce. By those tests, this proposal pairs one genuinely cheap and effective instrument (default enrollment into a no-fee account) with one expensive and largely ineffective instrument (a 200 percent match rate layered on top of an already-enacted 50 percent match), and the budget is dominated by the second.

All computations below are mine, run against the repository's row-level simulation frames (`code/01_inframarginal_decomposition.R` in this folder; outputs in `tables/` and `figures/`). I take the model's full-participation ceiling of $24.9 billion as the disciplined basis for decomposition, since the headline $14.9 billion simply scales it by an assumed 59.8 percent take-up.

## 1. Where the dollars go: the inframarginal problem

Of the 46.07 million workers eligible under the proposal, 9.56 million (20.8 percent) **already participate in a defined-contribution plan**, and another 6.43 million (14.0 percent) already hold a DC account they are not currently funding. At full participation, $5.01 billion (20.1 percent) of the $24.9 billion ceiling flows to the already-participating group and a further $3.30 billion (13.2 percent) to existing account holders — one-third of the program ($8.3 billion) lands on workers who already possess the account infrastructure and, in the first group, the contribution habit the program is supposed to create (`tables/inframarginal_decomposition.csv`; `figures/fig1_ceiling_cost_decomposition.png`, left bar). The already-participating group is also the highest-income slice of the eligible population (mean MAGI $46,849, versus $35,105 for those with no DC account), so the windfall skews up the income distribution within the eligibility band.

The arithmetic of "cost per saver actually reached" follows directly. The ceiling works out to $541 per eligible worker, but $683 of total program cost per worker **not** already participating in a DC plan (36.50 million workers) — and that calculation charitably assumes every one of them becomes a saver (`figures/fig2_cost_per_new_saver.png`). Under the headline behavioral assumption, only about 60 percent do, and the simulation itself warns that take-up among the 4.41 million self-employed eligibles is likely overstated because no payroll mechanism exists for them.

## 2. The marginal cost of expansion: 80 percent of the increment is a rate increase, not new reach

The sharper test compares the proposal against the enacted §6433 baseline (33.08 million eligible, $9.19 billion at full participation, $278 average match — the `universal_m100` row of `output/tables/main/sm_jct_replication_scenarios.xlsx`, which my script reproduces exactly from `data/processed/sipp_modeled.parquet`). The hybrid's eligibility band is a strict superset of current law: every current-law-eligible worker remains eligible, and 12.98 million workers are added at the frontier.

Decomposing the $15.73 billion full-participation increment (`tables/marginal_expansion_decomposition.csv`; `figures/fig1_ceiling_cost_decomposition.png`, right bar):

| Component | Workers (M) | Dollars ($B) |
|---|---|---|
| Current-law Saver's Match dollars (universal_m100 baseline) | 33.08 | 9.19 |
| (a) Rate increase for current-law-eligible workers — pure transfer | 33.08 | 12.56 |
| (b) Frontier expansion: dollars to newly eligible workers | 12.98 | 3.17 |
| **Hybrid full-participation ceiling** | **46.07** | **24.92** |

**Seventy-nine point eight percent of the incremental cost ($12.56 billion of $15.73 billion) is a rate increase for workers already eligible under current law.** These workers already face a 50 percent federal match on the first $2,000 contributed; the proposal raises their subsidy rate toward 200 percent without extending eligibility to a single additional person. Only $3.17 billion — 20.2 percent of the increment — buys the frontier expansion, and even there 4.11 million of the 12.98 million newly eligible workers (31.6 percent, receiving $1.11 billion) already participate in a DC plan today (`tables/cross_decomposition_ceiling_cost.csv`).

The marginal-cost curve is therefore steeply rising: current law delivers an eligible worker at $278; the proposal's incremental eligible worker costs $1,212 — 4.4 times as much — because each new entrant at the frontier drags a rate increase for the entire infra-frontier population behind him (`figures/fig2_cost_per_new_saver.png`). That is the signature of a poorly targeted expansion: the program grows by deepening subsidies where it already operates, not by reaching where it does not.

One honest caveat cuts the other way. Realized current-law spending will be far below the $9.2 billion full-participation construct — JCT scored SECURE 2.0 §103 at roughly $2.1 billion for FY2028 (JCT, "Estimated Budget Effects of the Revenue Provisions of Division T," JCX-21-22, 2022, in repo at `Infrastructure/references/literature/papers/x-21-22_jct_jcx-21-22.pdf`; the repo's replication table carries the same figure), precisely because the opt-in, access-gated design reaches few people. Relative to *realized* current law, nearly the whole $14.9 billion headline is new money. That makes the decomposition more important, not less: if Congress is to spend roughly seven times the JCT-scored §103 outlay, it should know that most of the gap is rate generosity, not coverage.

## 3. What the crowd-out literature says a 200 percent match buys

The best evidence we have on price subsidies for retirement saving is devastating to the match-rate margin. Using Danish administrative data on 41 million observations, Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen find that each $1 of government expenditure on retirement-saving subsidies raised total saving by about **1 cent**, because roughly 85 percent of individuals are passive savers who do not respond to prices, while the active responders largely shift assets between taxable and tax-favored accounts (Chetty et al., "Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 129(3), 2014, https://www.nber.org/papers/w18565.). Automatic contributions, by contrast, raised total saving substantially with little offset. This finding did not come from nowhere: the 1990s debate between Engen, Gale, and Scholz — who argued IRA and 401(k) subsidies were largely reshuffled saving ("The Illusory Effects of Saving Incentives on Saving," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 10(4), 1996; NBER WP 5759, https://www.nber.org/papers/w5759.) — and Poterba, Venti, and Wise, who read the same data as mostly new saving ("How Retirement Saving Programs Increase Saving," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 10(4), 1996, https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/jep.10.4.91.pdf.), was effectively adjudicated by the Danish data in favor of the skeptics on the *price* margin and the optimists on the *default* margin.

The proposal's own architecture concedes the point. Its effective instrument is the universal default account with a 3 percent automatic contribution — the passive-saver mechanism Chetty et al. validate. But its budget is dominated by the price instrument: raising the match rate from 50 percent to as much as 200 percent. The H&R Block field experiment — the cleanest evidence on match rates for exactly this population — found that even a 50 percent match raised IRA take-up only from 3 percent to 14 percent among low- and middle-income tax filers (Duflo, Gale, Liebman, Orszag, and Saez, "Saving Incentives for Low- and Middle-Income Families: Evidence from a Field Experiment with H&R Block," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 121(4), 2006; NBER WP 11680, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11680.). Match-rate elasticities are real but small; defaults do the work. A 200 percent rate — double the most generous employer match in common private practice (typically 50 to 100 percent on a limited base) and quadruple the enacted §6433 rate — purchases very little additional saving behavior per dollar. At that rate the policy is barely a "match" at all: a $500 contribution triggers the full $1,000 federal deposit, which is functionally a near-demogrant gated by a token contribution. If Congress wants to transfer $1,000 to low-income households, it should debate that transfer on its merits, not launder it through saving-incentive language that the evidence says will generate about a penny of net new private saving per subsidy dollar.

The phaseout geometry adds a quiet distortion: because the match rate falls linearly with MAGI (150 percentage points over the $32,879 Single pivot), a Single filer contributing $1,000 loses roughly $46 of match per additional $1,000 of MAGI — an implicit marginal tax of about 4.6 percentage points stacked on top of existing phaseouts (EITC, SNAP, premium credits) in exactly the income range where cumulative marginal rates are already highest. And for the lowest earners the $1,000 cap binds at a $500 contribution, so the marginal incentive to save *beyond* $500 is zero precisely where the headline rate is largest.

## 4. Indexing to the median: a built-in growth ratchet

Current law indexes the §6433 thresholds to CPI from 2028 (IRS, Notice 2024-65, 2024, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-65.pdf.). The proposal instead anchors its pivots to the Single weighted-median MAGI, re-anchored on each SIPP wave. That is wage indexing in all but name: because real wages grow, the eligibility frontier — and therefore enrollment and cost — grows permanently relative to a CPI-indexed baseline. This is the same design choice that drives the long-run growth of initial Social Security benefits, and it should be scored as such. A program advertised at $14.9 billion with a $24.9 billion ceiling and a wage-indexed frontier is, over a budget window, a $150 to $250 billion commitment with an embedded escalator and no official JCT or CBO score. The repository itself flags ±15 percent uncertainty from the SIPP MAGI proxy alone, unmodeled §6433(d)(2) distribution recapture (which any serious draft must enforce, since a 200 percent rate otherwise invites contribute-withdraw cycling), and no employer-response modeling.

## 5. The political economy: §6433's real flaw, and what the EO already fixes

The Saver's Match passed in 2022 because it was a modest, bipartisan conversion of a failed tax credit — the Saver's Credit reached about 5 percent of taxpayers, with claiming nearly impossible at the bottom because the credit was nonrefundable (NIRS, "Improving the Saver's Credit for Low- and Moderate-Income Workers," 2017, https://www.nirsonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/final_savers_credit_report_sept_2017.pdf.; CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). The binding flaw in §6433 is not the 50 percent rate; it is delivery — the match must land in an account, plans are not required to accept it, and no mechanism creates accounts or contributions for the unenrolled (IRS Notice 2024-65; IRSAC's January 2026 warning that success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions," per ASPPA, 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.).

The April 30, 2026 executive order attacks exactly that delivery problem at near-zero fiscal cost: by January 1, 2027, TrumpIRA.gov must list IRA providers that accept §6433 match deposits, cap net expense ratios at 0.15 percent, and impose no minimums (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.). I share AEI's skepticism about whether a government comparison site adds much to existing private infrastructure (Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.), but as a fiscal matter the order plus the enacted match gets the deposit-destination fix and the §104 awareness function for the cost of a website. What it cannot do — and what the Chetty evidence says actually matters — is auto-enrollment. The honest gap between the EO and this proposal is therefore the default-contribution architecture, which my own decomposition prices at the $9.2 billion universal_m100 counterfactual (universal access at current-law match parameters), not $24.9 billion. The access fix delivers the coverage; the extra $15.7 billion mostly buys rate.

## 6. Against the RSAA comparison

The RSAA (S.1526/H.R.2696) keys its match to the worker's **contribution rate** — 100 percent on the first 3 percent of income, 50 percent on the next 2 percent — which keeps a live price at the saving margin across the whole schedule and scales the federal cost with actual contribution effort (S.1526 bill text, 119th Congress, https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1526.). The hybrid keys the match *rate* to MAGI with a flat $1,000 cap, which kills the contribution margin at $500 for the poorest workers and converts the subsidy into a quasi-demogrant. On pure incentive design, RSAA's structure is better; on fiscal exposure, it is worse — its 5 percent-of-income maximum federal contribution plus a 1 percent non-contingent deposit grows with wages and has no per-person dollar cap, and Morningstar's simulations warn it would *reduce* projected retirement wealth 12 to 20 percent for younger cohorts by crowding workers out of higher-contribution employer plans (Look and VanDerhei, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," Morningstar, 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.). Both bills share the deeper crowd-out risk: a subsidized federal account gives small employers a reason to drop plans. Neither has a JCT score. I decline to endorse either match design; the account architecture is the only element of either worth keeping.

## Verdict: Oppose as designed

The universal default account is good policy that the evidence supports. The match expansion stapled to it is not: 80 percent of the incremental cost is a rate increase for workers already eligible under current law, one-third of ceiling dollars land on workers who already hold DC accounts, the 200 percent rate exceeds any private-market analog and buys roughly a penny of net new saving per dollar by the best available estimates, and the median-anchored pivots build permanent program growth into a proposal with no official score.

**Top three recommendations:**

1. **Decouple the access architecture from the rate increase.** Enact the universal default account and auto-enrollment at the *enacted* §6433 parameters (50 percent rate, current thresholds). My replication prices that counterfactual at $9.2 billion at full participation — it captures the entire 33.08 million-worker current-law population and the coverage mechanism, at 37 percent of the proposal's ceiling.
2. **Replace median-MAGI anchoring with the statute's CPI indexing**, and require a JCT score with explicit crowd-out and employer-response margins before any rate parameter moves above 50 percent. If additional generosity survives that scoring, key it to the contribution rate (RSAA-style) rather than to MAGI, so the marginal price of saving stays alive past the first $500.
3. **Model and enforce §6433(d)(2) distribution recapture before legislating any rate above 100 percent.** At a 200 percent match, contribute-and-withdraw cycling is a first-order gaming margin; the simulation does not model recapture, and the proposal should not be priced as if behavior at a 2:1 riskless return mirrors behavior at 1:2.
