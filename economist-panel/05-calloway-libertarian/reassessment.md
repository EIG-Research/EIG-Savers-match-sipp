# Re-Review 05 — Dr. Jack Calloway (Free-Market / Libertarian Public Economics)

**Reviewing:** the revised hybrid proposal (`Infrastructure/specs/2026-05-28_universal-account-savers-match-hybrid-proposal.md`, refreshed 2026-06-11) against my first-round assessment (verdict: oppose as drafted). All figures below are the current canonical numbers (`_shared/data-guide.md`); my own spot-check (`code/04_rereview_federal_flow.R`) reproduced the 44.47M eligible (30.6 percent of the 145.34M universe), the $23.87B ceiling, and the 25.98M/18.49M routing split to the dollar before anything else was computed.

## 1. Disposition check

**"Kill the median re-anchoring (CPI + sunset)" → Adopted.** I accept the reading, with one reservation the changelog itself flags honestly: what was adopted is an IRS single-filer anchor, C-CPI-U indexing, and a *decennial re-anchor*. I asked for a CPI index plus a *ten-year sunset with mandatory JCT re-scoring at reauthorization*. A re-anchor and a sunset run on the same calendar but with opposite defaults, and in political economy the default is the whole game. Call it three-quarters adopted. Detail in Section 2.

**"No federal account; use EO marketplace / state rails" → Declined.** Accurate reading. I contest the rationale below.

**"Liquidity / clawback-free emergencies; pilot first" → Partial.** Accurate, and I will say plainly that D6 is better than what I asked for: own contributions Roth-accessible with hardship withdrawals and loans drawn from them, and the §6433(d)(2) three-year recapture — the "machine for converting federal transfers into penalty revenue" in my first round — replaced wholesale by a six-month match forfeiture with no tax penalty on the distribution itself. That is a clean, administrable anti-churn rule, and I concede it fully. The pilot remains open (S2); it migrates into my conditions.

## 2. The declines

**D2, the federal account.** The stated rationale — worker-level portability avoids the churn, cash-out, and vesting erosion of employer-tied accounts — is a good argument against the *wrong alternative*. I never proposed employer-tied accounts. An individually owned IRA opened through the EO marketplace or a state auto-IRA is worker-level and portable *by construction*: it is the worker's own §408 account, it follows her across jobs, and it vests instantly because there is no employer in the chain. Portability distinguishes individual accounts from employer plans; it does not distinguish a Treasury-run individual account from a privately custodied one. So the rationale answers Bridger's behavioral point and leaves my objection untouched.

And my objection was never about portability; it was about concentration. I re-ran the routing on the current base: **57.32 million workers — 39.4 percent of the entire universe — default into the federal account** (the re-anchoring moved the eligibility band, not the channel routing, so this is essentially the round-one figure). At the 3 percent default, the federal-account inflow (contributions plus match deposits) is **$85.6 billion per year at the 59 percent conservative rung, $116.1 billion at the 80 percent central case, and $145.1 billion at full participation — two to three times the entire TSP's audited CY2023 inflow of $44.7 billion**, on day one, compounding thereafter. The TSP governance record (China-divestment bills from one party, ESG bolt-ons from the other) tells you what happens to a pool that size. The political cost is "accepted knowingly," per D2. Noted — but the cost is not the author's to accept. It lands on 57 million default enrollees whose savings become a permanent legislative temptation. The objection stands.

**The ratchet, revisited under C-CPI-U + decennial re-anchor.** Here the design genuinely moved, and I will give it its due. The old SIPP-wave re-anchoring held eligibility at roughly a third of the workforce *forever, by formula*; the new regime lets the eligible share drift down with real wage growth between re-anchorings — current law's self-sunsetting property, slowed — and the ten-year arithmetic shows it: the growth premium falls from ~19.5 percent (wage-indexed) to ~13 percent, and the 80 percent headline runs **~$216 billion over FY2027–FY2036** (range $191B flat to $228B wage-indexed; ~$24B/yr by year ten; conservative ~$160B, ceiling ~$270B). Indexing the $1,000 cap to C-CPI-U also dissolves the dynamic I flagged in round one — a growing majority pinned at a binding nominal cap, lobbying to raise it (my old $24.9B→$36.2B creep projection is superseded with the design that produced it). Both are real improvements.

But the decennial re-anchor reintroduces the ratchet at decade frequency. Every ten years, eligibility snaps back to the income distribution *automatically* — no vote, no score, no fingerprints. Congress's inaction restores full coverage; under a sunset, Congress's inaction ends the program. The one-line-amendment risk also survives: the floor, the cap, the ⅔ calibration, and the default rate remain single reconciliation sentences, and the re-anchor guarantees the base they multiply against never erodes for longer than a decade. The slower index answers half my concern; the automatic re-anchor un-answers the other half. The fix is cheap: keep the exact decennial calendar, but make the re-anchor a *reauthorization* with a mandatory JCT re-score — same numbers, opposite default.

## 3. Fresh eyes — the five focus questions

**F1, the 200 percent floor.** D3's candor is admirable: the model assumes match-invariant participation, so its "no new savers" result cannot adjudicate the floor — but symmetry cuts both ways, and the bet rests on a literature that mostly runs against it. Match-rate experiments (the H&R Block field experiment; the broader match-elasticity work) find participation responds far more to defaults and framing than to the match rate; the default in this design does the heavy lifting, and the floor is expensive signaling layered on top. I concede the cost is structurally bounded — the $1,000 cap means the floor mainly lowers the contribution needed to reach it. My advice: keep S2, but give it teeth — legislate the randomized 50/100/200 arm with the floor *sunsetting to 100 percent* unless the evaluation finds a participation or persistence effect. Evidence should ratchet generosity up; the statute should not presume it.

**F2, the federal account.** Covered above. The marketplace alternative delivers the portability D2 wants without the $86–145 billion-a-year honeypot. If the federal account survives anyway, the non-negotiables are statutory: an independent fiduciary board, an index-only menu, and an explicit prohibition on non-pecuniary investment criteria — none of which RSAA's bare architecture guarantees against a future rider.

**F3, the anchor population.** The IRS anchor is the right call — published, replicable, scorable without SIPP. But be honest about what ⅔ is: a calibration reverse-engineered so the new anchor reproduces the old coverage (44.47M vs. 46.07M, within ~3–4 percent), then dressed as a legislator-legible principle. I prefer the honesty inverted: legislate the *dollar pivots* the calibration produces ($32,235 / $48,353 / $64,471, C-CPI-U-indexed) and let the "two-thirds of the median" live in the committee report as derivation, not in the statute as formula. One genuine curiosity for the record: the filer median is endogenous to the program. A refundable match that pulls low-income nonfilers into filing lowers the single-filer median at the next re-anchor, which *shrinks* the band — fiscally favorable, analytically untidy, and another small argument for fixing dollars in statute rather than chaining the law to a statistic the law itself moves.

**F4, the 80 percent framing.** Foregrounding $19.09B over $14.15B imports employer-payroll auto-enrollment stickiness into a channel that has never existed — a Treasury account absorbing 58.4 percent of eligibles, including 4.41M self-employed workers with no payroll to deduct from, where take-up runs through estimated-tax elections and platform rails. State auto-IRA evidence (persistence ≈0.59, Tier 2.2) points down, not up. The conservative $14.15B should lead every external statement, with 80 percent labeled an upside scenario contingent on rail-specific evidence that does not yet exist. On the open Tier-2 assumptions, my advice for the record: the 0.59 persistence anchor is defensible, arguably mildly pessimistic for a *matched* account (state programs are unmatched); the W-2 default rate belongs at 75–85 percent, the self-employed rate in the single digits to ~20 percent absent auto-debit; the six-month forfeiture bound should assume 5–10 percent of matched contributions withdrawn inside the window (the leakage literature's $0.40-per-dollar is a lifetime figure, not a six-month one); and ~2.7 percent C-CPI-U is a fine central path.

**F5, debt financing.** No. A directional citation to RAND's analysis of a *different and much larger program* breaking even in 30–45 years is not a pay-for; it is an IOU written against a model. Worse, the offset contains a contradiction the draft should not paper over: it works only if account balances *count* against SSI/Medicaid asset tests and delay eligibility — yet the predictable advocacy (and good poverty policy) is to *exclude* these balances from asset tests. You cannot book the offset and grant the exclusion. Pick one, in the draft. If the program needs $19–24 billion a year, the coherent source is inside the retirement tax expenditure itself — Okafor's DC-exclusion envelope. I rarely endorse cap-the-exclusion arguments, but financing a bottom-targeted match by trimming a top-skewed subsidy is fiscally honest in a way ten more years of borrowing is not.

## 4. Updated verdict

**Oppose as drafted — but moved, materially.** Round one was opposition to the architecture root and branch: a permanent formula-driven entitlement, a clawback trap, and a Treasury asset manager at the center. The revision fixed the first substantially (C-CPI-U, indexed cap, slower path), fixed the second outright (D6, better than my own ask), and kept the third deliberately. What remains is conditional opposition, three conditions from support with modifications:

1. **Route the default through the EO marketplace / state auto-IRA rails instead of a federal account** — or, at minimum, statutory insulation: independent board, index-only menu, prohibition on non-pecuniary criteria.
2. **Convert the decennial re-anchor into decennial reauthorization with mandatory JCT re-scoring.** Same calendar, opposite default. This is the cheapest concession on the table and it completes the disposition the changelog credits me with.
3. **Legislate the S2 evaluation with consequences attached** — the 200 percent floor sunsets to 100 percent absent demonstrated behavioral payoff — and replace the RAND gesture with an explicit pay-for.

Grant the second and third and I am at support-with-modifications even swallowing the federal account, provided its guardrails are statutory. Grant none and the proposal remains what I said in round one, only better-indexed: a well-aimed subsidy bolted to an architecture built to outlive every promise made about its size.
