# Panel Assessment 05 — Dr. Jack Calloway (Free-Market / Libertarian Public Economics)

**Proposal under review:** `Infrastructure/specs/2026-05-28_universal-account-savers-match-hybrid-proposal.md` (universal default account + MAGI-linear Saver's Match redesign).
**Date:** 2026-06-10. **Verdict: Oppose as drafted** (constructive alternatives below).

I evaluate retirement policy from a classical-liberal vantage: skepticism of new federal programs and the ratchets built into them, attention to the politicization risk of government-managed assets, and a presumption that the burden of proof sits with the intervention, not the market. I have recomputed every number below from the repository's own row-level SIPP frames (`code/01_indexing_ratchet.R`, `code/02_ratchet_menu.R`, `code/03_federal_concentration.R`); where the data support the proposal's diagnosis, I say so.

## 1. What the proposal gets right — and why honesty requires saying it first

The access-gap diagnosis is real. The enacted Saver's Match (SECURE 2.0 §103, IRC §6433) is a refundable credit that must land in a retirement account the statute does not create, plans are not required to accept the deposits, and Treasury has issued no proposed regulations eighteen months before the first match year (IRS, "Notice 2024-65," 2024, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-65.pdf.). The IRS's own advisory council warns that the program's success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions" (ASPPA, "Prioritize the Saver's Match, Advisory Council Tells IRS," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.). The predecessor Saver's Credit reached 5.7 percent of taxpayers with an average benefit of $191 (Warshawsky, AEI, "TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.). A match with no rail to run on may simply fail. §6433 passed precisely because it was a bipartisan, modest, capped, *opt-in* redesign folded into an omnibus (CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.); the political economy lesson the proposal draws — add an account and a default — is one Congress deliberately declined to enact. That refusal was not an oversight. It was the price of passage.

## 2. The indexing ratchet: a program designed never to sunset

The proposal anchors its match-rate pivots to the SIPP weighted-median MAGI and re-anchors them "on each new SIPP wave." This is the most consequential sentence in the document, and it receives no fiscal discussion. I quantified it (`figures/fig1_indexing_ratchet.png`; `tables/indexing_ratchet_paths.csv`).

Current law is, by construction, a *self-sunsetting* program. Its dollar thresholds are fixed through TY2027 and CPI-indexed thereafter; because wages outgrow prices, eligibility erodes mechanically. Using the repository's own projection factor (1.093 per roughly three years of wage growth): with 2024 incomes, 38.54 million workers (26.5 percent of the 145.34 million-worker universe) fall under the current-law upper thresholds; with the same incomes projected to TY2027, 33.08 million (22.8 percent). **One wave of wage growth removes 5.45 million workers — 3.8 percentage points of the workforce — from eligibility.** Projecting forward with CPI-indexed thresholds and wages outpacing CPI by one percentage point per year, the eligible share falls from 22.8 percent in 2027 to 15.3 percent by 2051; with frozen nominal thresholds it falls to 7.0 percent.

The hybrid inverts this. Re-anchoring pivots to the median holds the eligible share at **31.69 percent of all workers in every wave, forever** — the red horizontal line in Figure 1. The mirror-image check: against 2024 incomes, the 2027-anchored pivots would cover 52.26 million workers (36.0 percent); re-anchoring trims that to exactly 46.07 million (31.7 percent). Eligibility is no longer a poverty-relief boundary that prosperity shrinks; it is a fixed claim on roughly one-third of the workforce as a permanent constituency. Worse, the ratchet is asymmetric in cost: because the $1,000 cap is fixed in nominal dollars while contributions grow with wages, the full-participation cost under re-anchoring creeps from $24.9 billion to $36.2 billion by 2051 as the share of eligible workers hitting the cap rises from 12.6 percent to 56.9 percent (`tables/indexing_ratchet_paths.csv`) — and a majority of beneficiaries pinned at a binding nominal cap is precisely the constituency that lobbies to raise it. Median indexing converts a transition subsidy into a permanent entitlement, and it does so silently, in a re-anchoring formula no appropriator will ever vote on.

## 3. The ratchet menu: every parameter is one reconciliation bill away

The proposal's revision note concedes that the *first draft* used a 300 percent floor with pivots at the full Single-filer median, later "pulled back" to 200 percent and 0.6 times the median. Parameters that moved once in drafting will move again in committee. I re-priced one-line parameter changes from the row-level data — `min(rate(MAGI) × default × earnings, cap)` — in `tables/ratchet_menu.csv` and `figures/fig2_ratchet_menu.png`:

| Scenario | Eligible (M) | Share of workers | Full-participation cost | Headline (59.8% take-up) | Multiple of baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. As proposed ($1,000 cap, 200% floor) | 46.07 | 31.7% | $24.9B | $14.9B | 1.00× |
| 2. Cap to $1,500 | 46.07 | 31.7% | $26.4B | $15.8B | 1.06× |
| 3. Cap to $2,000 | 46.07 | 31.7% | $26.7B | $16.0B | 1.07× |
| 4. Default contribution 3% → 5% | 46.07 | 31.7% | $33.9B | $20.3B | 1.36× |
| 5. Floor to 300% (pivots held) | 39.60 | 27.2% | $25.9B | $15.5B | 1.04× |
| 6. First-draft design (300% floor, pivots at full Single median) | 74.76 | 51.4% | $62.2B | $37.2B | 2.50× |
| 7. First-draft design + $2,000 cap | 74.76 | 51.4% | $89.3B | $53.4B | 3.58× |

Two honest observations cut in different directions. Cap increases alone are cheap (rows 2–3) because the 200 percent floor already lets low earners reach $1,000 on small contributions, and raising the floor with pivots held (row 5) actually *narrows* the eligibility band — the schedule's geometry pulls the zero-match endpoint inward. So the proposal as drawn is not maximally fragile at every margin, and I concede that. But the realistic legislative package is row 6: the proposal's own first draft, which covers **74.76 million workers — 51.4 percent of the workforce — at $62.2 billion per year at full participation, 2.5 times the baseline**; add the "restore parity with §6433's $2,000 base" amendment and it is $89.3 billion, 3.6 times. The distance between the reviewed proposal and a program costing two-and-a-half to three-and-a-half times as much is a few words in a reconciliation title, with the advocacy infrastructure (a universal account, an existing match formula, 46 million enrolled claimants) already in place. That is how program-growth ratchets work: SECURE 1.0 began the auto-enrollment march in 2019; SECURE 2.0 mandated it for new plans in 2022 and created the match in the same bill.

## 4. The federal asset manager nobody scored

The routing table is the proposal's quietest large number (`tables/federal_routing.csv`, `tables/federal_flow_vs_tsp.csv`, `figures/fig3_federal_flow_vs_tsp.png`). **57.32 million workers — 39.4 percent of the entire universe, and 26.65 million (57.9 percent) of the match-eligible — are defaulted into the federal, Treasury-administered universal account.** At the 3 percent default, the contribution flow routed through that account is $78.4 billion per year at the headline take-up rate and $131.1 billion at full participation; adding federal match deposits brings the federal-account inflow to **$87.0 billion to $145.6 billion per year**. The audited benchmark: the entire Thrift Savings Plan took in $44.7 billion in CY2023 — $31.2 billion from 7.0 million participants and $13.6 billion from their employing agencies (FRTIB, "Thrift Savings Fund Financial Statements," 2024, https://www.frtib.gov/pdf/reading-room/FinStmts/TSP-FS-Dec2023.pdf.). The proposal builds a federal account with **two to three times the TSP's annual inflow and roughly five to eight times its contributing membership** on day one, compounding toward trillion-dollar scale within a decade or two.

The governance record of the *existing* federal account should end any complacency here. The TSP — the best-governed pool of retirement capital the federal government runs — is under permanent two-party siege: Republican bills to strip Chinese holdings from its index funds and Democratic bills to bolt on ESG funds and fossil-fuel screens (FedWeek, "China, ESG, DEI: Congress Eyeing TSP Investment Choices," 2024, https://www.fedweek.com/retirement-financial-planning/china-esg-dei-congress-eyeing-tsp-investment-choices/.). A fund holding the default savings of 57 million mostly low-income voters will be a vastly richer political prize. And the "automatic" architecture has an enforcement footprint the proposal never mentions: California's auto-IRA collects $250 per eligible employee from non-compliant employers, plus an additional $500 per employee at the final notice, annually until compliance, through the Franchise Tax Board (FTB, "CalSavers" collections page, 2026, https://www.ftb.ca.gov/pay/collections/calsavers/index.html.). Universal coverage means universal mandate; someone has to be fined.

## 5. Paternalism with other people's liquidity

Auto-enrollment is soft paternalism with hard costs for exactly the population this proposal targets. The U.S. DC system leaks: for every $1 contributed by savers under 55, roughly $0.40 flows out before retirement (Beshears, Choi, Hurwitz, Laibson, and Madrian, "Liquidity in Retirement Savings Systems: An International Comparison," NBER WP 21168, 2015, https://www.nber.org/papers/w21168.), and in 2010 taxable early distributions reached 45 cents per dollar contributed, with withdrawal events concentrated among lower-income taxpayers hit by income and marital shocks (Argento, Bryant, and Sabelhaus, "Early Withdrawals from Retirement Accounts During the Great Recession," FEDS 2013-22, 2013, https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2013/201322/201322pap.pdf.). Defaulting a liquidity-constrained worker into illiquid saving, matching the deposit, and then clawing back the match through §6433's recovery tax when she withdraws in an emergency (a mechanism flagged in Notice 2024-65) is a machine for converting federal transfers into penalty revenue and administrative churn. Choukhmane's structural estimates add the deeper point: auto-enrollment's long-run wealth effect is "negligible except at the bottom of the earnings distribution" because workers offset defaults by saving less later (Choukhmane, "Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics," 2021, https://tahachoukhmane.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Choukhmane-2021-Default-options.pdf.). I concede the flip side honestly: the bottom of the distribution is where this proposal aims, and it is the one place the default literature finds durable effects. But that is an argument for a narrow, liquid, well-piloted intervention — not for defaulting 57 million people into a Treasury fund with a withdrawal tax.

## 6. The executive order is the better instrument — with reservations

The April 30, 2026 executive order and TrumpIRA.gov (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.) attack the same deposit-destination problem with the architecture I prefer: a comparison marketplace of *private* IRA providers who accept §6433 match deposits, with listing conditioned on no minimums and net fees below 0.15 percent. No accounts are created, no one is defaulted, no statute is bent. My reservations run in both directions. The fee cap is a price control by another name — Warshawsky notes only about 6.4 percent of target-date funds clear it, so the "marketplace" may open nearly empty (AEI, 2026.) — and a government website blessing specific firms is itself a politicization vector. And I will not overclaim its efficacy: an information portal grafted onto an opt-in credit whose predecessor reached 5.7 percent of taxpayers will likely move take-up modestly. The EO does not solve the access gap; it proves the gap can be narrowed without a new federal program, and its Section 6 codification recommendations are where this fight will actually occur.

## 7. RSAA: same church, bigger collection plate

Measured against the Retirement Savings for Americans Act (S.1526/H.R.2696), the hybrid is in some respects the more disciplined design: its $1,000 per-person nominal cap bounds per-capita exposure, whereas RSAA's match is up to 4 percent of income plus a **1 percent automatic federal contribution conditioned on nothing** — a pure demogrant inside a retirement wrapper — with no JCT or CBO score in three Congresses. Both, however, index eligibility to the income distribution (RSAA phases out at 100/150/200 percent of median income), so both carry the permanence ratchet of Section 2; both build the federal default account of Section 4; and the best independent simulations find RSAA *reduces* projected retirement wealth about 20 percent for Gen Z and 12 percent for millennials through crowd-out of more generous private DC saving (Look and VanDerhei, Morningstar, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.; see also RAND, "Impacts of the Retirement Savings for Americans Act," RRA2614-3, 2024, https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2614-3.html.). The hybrid's sharper bottom-targeting (200 percent floor, MAGI-keyed) buys better incidence per dollar than RSAA, and its repository candidly concedes employer responses are unmodeled. From my chair: RSAA is the more fiscally dangerous bill; the hybrid is the more *politically* dangerous design, because median indexing plus a universal default account is the cleaner template for permanence.

## Verdict: Oppose as drafted

The diagnosis is sound; the instrument is a permanent, self-perpetuating federal program with an unpriced expansion menu and a Treasury asset manager twice the TSP's size at its center. My recommendations, in order:

1. **Kill the median re-anchoring.** Set statutory dollar pivots indexed to CPI (chained), exactly as §6433 does, and add a 10-year sunset with mandatory JCT re-scoring at reauthorization. If Congress wants to cover 32 percent of workers forever, make it vote to do so, on the record, each decade. (Figure 1 shows precisely what is being decided by formula instead.)
2. **No federal account.** Route the default through the EO's private-provider marketplace and existing state auto-IRA rails, with an independent fiduciary board, an index-only statutory menu, and an explicit prohibition on non-pecuniary investment criteria — and drop the fee price-cap in favor of disclosure plus competition. The $87–146 billion annual flow in Figure 3 should never sit one appropriations rider away from an ESG screen or a China divestment order.
3. **Respect liquidity constraints and pilot first.** Lower the default to 2 percent below 200 percent of poverty, deliver any match clawback-free through an emergency-withdrawal carve-out (the leakage literature says the withdrawals will happen regardless), and run the design as a scored, capped pilot in the 2027–2030 window — current law's own threshold erosion (5.45 million workers per wave, Section 2) guarantees the program shrinks gracefully if Congress does nothing, which is exactly the right default for an unproven $15–25 billion entitlement.
