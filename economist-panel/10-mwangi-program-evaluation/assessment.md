## 1. What the data show: the access gate, not the match rate, zeroes out the bottom

The single most important fact about the enacted Saver's Match (IRC §6433) is not its 50 percent rate; it is that the match is payable only into an existing qualifying account, and nothing in the statute or current guidance creates an account for the eligible worker who lacks one (IRS, "Notice 2024-65," 2024, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-65.pdf.). I quantified what that gate does, comparing three regimes worker by worker in the repo's 145.34-million-worker universe (`code/01_access_gate_contrast.R`, `figures/fig1_access_gate_three_regimes.png`, `tables/match_by_magi_bin_three_regimes.csv`):

- **(A) Current law if access were universal** (the repo's `univ_sm_match_m100` column): $9.19 billion in annual match offers at full participation.
- **(B) Current law as it will actually operate** — the same schedule, but zero for workers with no defined-contribution account to receive the deposit: $3.68 billion. The deposit-destination gate alone destroys 60 percent of the offered dollars.
- **(C) The hybrid proposal**: $24.92 billion at full participation.

The incidence of the gate is the story. In the bottom MAGI decile of the universe (MAGI below roughly $26,900 in TY2027 dollars), only 23.1 percent of workers hold a DC account. The mean match offer per bottom-decile worker is $252 under current law with universal access, $77 under current law as enacted, and $630 under the hybrid. In the $10,000–$15,000 MAGI bin the figures are $229, $77, and $643. Of the hybrid's 46.07 million eligible workers, 30.07 million — 65.3 percent — have no DC account today, so under current law they face a statutory match rate of 50 percent and an *effective* offer of zero unless they clear the burden of opening an IRA themselves. Figure 1 is the visual core of this assessment: the red (as-enacted) line is nearly flat at the bottom of the distribution, not because the law is stingy there, but because the law has nowhere to send the money.

## 2. What the causal evidence says matches and defaults buy

The proposal's central behavioral bet is that a 200 percent match plus a default account will create savers. The experimental record lets us price each component (`code/02_effect_size_benchmark.R`, `figures/fig2_dose_response_evidence_gap.png`).

**Matches, on the opt-in margin, are weak.** The only large-scale randomized test of match rates on IRA take-up is the H&R Block experiment: take-up was 3 percent in the control group, 8 percent at a 20 percent match, and 14 percent at a 50 percent match; average contributions (including non-contributors) were four and seven times control levels (Duflo, Gale, Liebman, Orszag, and Saez, "Saving Incentives for Low- and Middle-Income Families: Evidence from a Field Experiment with H&R Block," NBER Working Paper 11680, 2006, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11680.). A several-fold increase, on a 3 percent base, still leaves 86 percent of the offered population out. The implied dose-response is 0.22 percentage points of take-up per percentage point of match. A linear extrapolation to the proposal's 200 percent floor predicts roughly 47 percent opt-in take-up — and that extrapolation runs four times beyond the experimental support, against the grain of the paper's own concave pattern. No experiment anywhere has tested a match above 50 percent at scale.

**Defaults are strong.** Automatic enrollment raised new-hire 401(k) participation from roughly 37 percent to 86 percent — about 50 percentage points (Madrian and Shea, "The Power of Suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) Participation and Savings Behavior," NBER Working Paper 7682, 2000, https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682.). And the Danish registry evidence shows why: about 85 percent of savers are passive; each $1 of government expenditure on price subsidies raised total saving by approximately one cent, while automatic contributions raised wealth accumulation substantially (Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen, "Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark," NBER Working Paper 18565, 2012, https://www.nber.org/papers/w18565.).

I priced the three designs on the repo's own data, holding the eligible population at the hybrid's 46.07 million (9.56 million already participating; 36.5 million current non-savers). The table is produced by my code (`tables/cost_per_marginal_new_saver.csv`); for the default scenarios I reproduce the repo's exact headline mechanics (row-level observed participation for account holders; the 59.8 percent SIPP-observed conditional rate for the account-less), which replicate the published headline to the dollar (27.55 million participants, $14.95 billion).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i-a) Match-only, opt-in, take-up at largest tested match (14 percent gross) | 4.02 | 7.80 | $1,942 | 72 percent |
| (i-b) Match-only, opt-in, linear extrapolation to 200 percent match (47 percent gross) | 16.06 | 14.37 | $895 | 39 percent |
| (ii) Default-only: universal account + auto-enrollment, current-law §6433 match | 17.98 | 5.29 | $294 | 38 percent |
| (iii) The proposal: default account + auto-enrollment + hybrid match | 17.98 | 14.95 | $831 | 34 percent |

Two findings deserve emphasis. First, the default-only design (ii) creates *more* new savers than even the heroic match-only extrapolation (i-b) — 17.98 versus 16.06 million — at roughly one-third the cost: $294 versus $895 per marginal new saver. Second, within the model's own logic, scenarios (ii) and (iii) create *identical* numbers of new savers, because the repo assumes participation is invariant to the match rate. The hybrid's extra $9.7 billion per year relative to (ii) buys zero additional savers in the simulation; it buys larger transfers per saver, concentrated at the bottom. That may be a perfectly defensible distributional objective — a refundable deposit is a well-targeted transfer that also builds assets — but it should be defended as a transfer, not sold as a participation lever. Ramnath's quasi-experimental autopsy of the predecessor credit points the same direction: taxpayers bunched their AGI to capture a higher credit rate, yet the credit rate had no statistically significant effect on retirement contributions (Ramnath, "Taxpayers' responses to tax-based incentives for retirement savings: Evidence from the Saver's Credit notch," Journal of Public Economics 101: 77–93, 2013, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272713000479.). Chetty et al.'s crowd-out result adds a further caution: among the minority of active savers, matched contributions partly displace other saving, so new *contributions* overstate new *saving*.

## 3. The Saver's Credit autopsy, and whether §6433 fixed the right failure

The proposal's motivation rests on the predecessor's take-up catastrophe, and the verified numbers bear it out. From 2006 to 2014 only about 3 to 5 percent of eligible filers claimed the Saver's Credit; 61 million returns had incomes low enough for the 50 percent rate, but because the credit was nonrefundable only about one-sixth of those filers could benefit at all (NIRS, "Issue Brief: Improving the Saver's Credit for Low- and Moderate-Income Workers," 2017, https://www.nirsonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/final_savers_credit_report_sept_2017.pdf.). As of 2021 only 5.7 percent of taxpayers claimed it, averaging $191 (Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.). Awareness remains the binding learning cost: only 51 percent of U.S. workers have heard of the credit at all (Transamerica Center for Retirement Studies, "Only Half of U.S. Workers Are Aware of a Tax Credit for Retirement Savers," 2025, https://www.transamericainstitute.org/in-the-news/press-releases/details/only-half-united-states-workers-aware-of-retirement-savers-tax-credit.). Stack the burdens in Herd–Moynihan terms: learn the credit exists, compute eligibility, file Form 8880 correctly, have tax liability to absorb a nonrefundable credit, and have already made a contribution to an account you already own. Each step sheds the population the credit was designed for (Herd, Hoynes, Michener, and Moynihan, "Introduction: Administrative Burden as a Mechanism of Inequality in Policy Implementation," RSF: The Russell Sage Foundation Journal of the Social Sciences 9(5): 1–30, 2023, https://www.rsfjournal.org/content/9/5/1.).

Section 6433 fixed two burdens — refundability and delivery as a deposit rather than a liability offset — and left the largest one intact. The deposit-destination problem (no account, no match) is precisely the failure mode the administrative-burden framework predicts: the policy conditions a benefit on a prior, costly, voluntary act by exactly the population least equipped to perform it. The IRS's own advisory council names plan and IRA acceptance of deposits as the central implementation risk (ASPPA, "Prioritize the Saver's Match, Advisory Council Tells IRS," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.). My Figure 1 prices that risk: $5.5 billion per year of offered match dollars — 60 percent of the current-law total — evaporate at the gate, overwhelmingly at the bottom.

**The April 2026 executive order is an information remedy for a structural problem.** TrumpIRA.gov, due January 1, 2027, is a listing-and-comparison portal: vetted IRAs with sub-0.15 percent fees, no minimums, and a commitment to accept §6433(e)(2)(C) deposits (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," April 30, 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.). It lowers learning costs (one place to compare) and some compliance costs (no minimums; deposit acceptance), and that is genuinely useful — the fee cap and acceptance criteria are the kind of supply-side standards the hybrid would also need. But it creates no account, no default, and no contribution. The H&R Block experiment is the cleanest available test of what information-plus-easy-opening achieves without a default: professional assistance at the moment of tax filing, an account opened on the spot — and 86 percent of the 50-percent-match group still declined. A website asking low-income workers to self-initiate is a weaker treatment than that. The Chetty evidence says the 85 percent who are passive will not show up. The EO's own Section 6, directing legislative recommendations for codification, implicitly concedes the point.

## 4. Political economy, the RSAA, and evaluability

Section 6433 passed because it rode a bipartisan omnibus (SECURE 2.0, Division T of P.L. 117-328) built from the Cardin–Portman lineage; its match conversion was scored, modest, and uncontroversial — and it is now stalled in implementation, with no proposed regulations as of June 2026 and a Treasury promotion report due July 1, 2026 (economist-panel/_shared/policy-context.md, 2026.). The hybrid is best read as the statutory completion of §6433: it keeps the refundable-deposit chassis and adds the two missing pieces — an automatic account and an automatic contribution — that the evidence says do the behavioral work.

Against the RSAA (S.1526/H.R.2696, 119th Congress), the hybrid shares the universal-account architecture and the 3 percent default but keys the match to MAGI rather than to the contribution rate. From an evaluation standpoint the hybrid is the more identifiable design: its sharp eligibility frontier (zero match at $43,839/$65,758/$87,678 by filing group) and its kinks at the pivots create regression-discontinuity and regression-kink designs *by construction*, whereas the RSAA's match (100 percent on the first 3 percent of income, 50 percent on the next 2 percent, phasing out at median income) varies smoothly with income in ways that confound design-based inference. The RSAA's 1 percent unconditional federal contribution also pays out regardless of behavior; the hybrid's dollars are at least conditioned on a contribution. Morningstar's simulation warning — that a federal 3 percent default could crowd workers out of higher-default private plans, lowering projected wealth for younger cohorts (Look and VanDerhei, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," Morningstar, 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.) — applies with similar force to the hybrid and is unmodeled in the repo.

**Honest uncertainties.** First, external validity: nothing in the experimental record supports predictions about a 200 percent match; the relevant margin under auto-enrollment is opt-out and opt-up, where match-rate evidence is essentially absent. Second, the headline 59.8 percent take-up is an analogy, not an estimate: it transplants the participation rate of workers who *chose* employers offering plans onto gig workers, the self-employed (4.41 million eligibles with no payroll mechanism at all), and the previously uncovered. Madrian–Shea-style stickiness suggests it could be higher; the self-employed channel suggests lower. The repo itself flags this honestly. Third, the simulation's assumption that participation is match-invariant means the proposal's distinctive feature — the 200 percent floor — has literally no behavioral payoff in the model that justifies it.

**The proposal is an evaluation opportunity, and should be legislated as one.** A staged rollout — randomized by tax-unit cohort, or geographically phased as the universal account spins up — would identify the participation effect of the default directly. The eligibility endpoints support RD on the match's intensive margin; the pivot kinks support RKD; and because Treasury delivers the match through tax data, outcomes (contributions, balances, persistence) are measurable in administrative records at near-zero marginal cost. A pilot could also randomize match generosity (50 versus 100 versus 200 percent floors) within the default architecture — the experiment the literature has never run, and the only way to learn whether the $9.7 billion premium over scenario (ii) buys any behavior at all.

## Verdict: Support with modifications

The universal default account is among the best-evidenced interventions in household finance, and the proposal correctly diagnoses the access gate that will otherwise strand the enacted Saver's Match. The match redesign, by contrast, outruns the causal evidence by a factor of four. My modifications:

1. **Keep the account and the 3 percent default; stage the match floor.** Launch at a 100 percent floor (still double current law) and authorize escalation to 200 percent contingent on evaluated participation effects, given that the model's own mechanics show the 200 percent floor adds cost ($831 versus $294 per marginal new saver against the default-only design) without adding savers.
2. **Legislate the evaluation.** Mandate a randomized or phased rollout with Treasury administrative-data reporting, pre-registered RD/RKD analyses at the eligibility endpoints and pivots, and a randomized match-generosity arm — the proposal's sharp frontier is a public good for learning and should not be wasted.
3. **Close the self-employed delivery gap and harden the deposit rails before scaling.** Specify the contribution mechanism for the 4.41 million self-employed eligibles (estimated-tax piggyback or return-time reconciliation), and make federal universal-account acceptance of match deposits automatic, so the Herd–Moynihan failure mode cannot reproduce itself inside the new architecture. The TrumpIRA.gov fee and no-minimum standards are worth keeping as the listing criteria for any private-side routing.
