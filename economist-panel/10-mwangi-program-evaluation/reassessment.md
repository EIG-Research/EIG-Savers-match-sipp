## 2. The declined staging recommendation (D3)

The rationale is partly mine, turned around: the simulation assumes match-invariant participation, so its "no new savers from the floor" result cannot adjudicate the floor. I said that first, and I stand by it — the model is silent. But silence is not symmetric, because the model is not the only evidence. Outside the model we have a randomized dose-response up to a 50 percent match (take-up 3/8/14 percent at 0/20/50 — Duflo et al. 2006), concave in the match rate, plus Ramnath's bunching-without-contributions result and Chetty et al.'s 85-percent-passive finding. Every piece of causal evidence we possess says the opt-in response to match generosity is weak and flattening; none of it speaks above 50 percent. The author's bet — a liquidity-constrained participation and persistence response at 200 percent that no study has tested — is the novel empirical claim, and **the burden of proof sits with the novel claim that costs money now, not with the staging-down position that tracks the evidence**. Recomputed on the current base: the default architecture alone with the current-law match (scenario ii) creates 17.25M new savers at $5.24B — $304 per marginal new saver — while the full proposal creates the same 17.25M at $14.15B, $820 each. The $8.9B annual premium buys larger transfers, not (in anything we can currently verify) more savers. A promised evaluation that is "not yet drafted" cannot carry evidentiary weight; it is a promissory note, and promissory notes do not shift burdens.

That said, I will not pretend staging up is costless. Match rates legislated low rarely escalate, whatever the trigger says; and the author is right that a 200 percent launch with the "$500 unlocks $1,000" frame is a cleaner behavioral message. So I convert my objection into a condition rather than holding it absolutely: **D3 is acceptable if and only if S2 is in the statute with a pre-specified de-escalation default** — if the evaluation does not find a participation/persistence payoff to the elevated floor by a named date, the floor steps down automatically at the next decennial re-anchor. That allocates the burden correctly even while launching at 200: the expensive parameter must earn its keep on the record, or it expires. An S2 that is merely authorized, unfunded, or discretionary does not satisfy the condition, and my recommendation reverts to staged escalation.

**What a credible S2 must contain** (focus question 1 — yes, a built-in evaluation is the right instrument, but only this kind):

- **A randomized match-generosity arm.** Randomize universal-account enrollee cohorts by tax unit across 50/100/200 percent floors for the first two to three enrollment years, with grandfathering onto the legislated schedule afterward. The federal account (D2) is what makes this administrable — Treasury controls the rail end to end. This is the experiment the literature has never run and the only direct test of the D3 bet. Primary outcomes, pre-registered: any-contribution participation, opt-out, opt-up above the 3 percent default, 12- and 24-month persistence, six-month forfeiture incidence, and crowd-out into/from other tax-visible saving.
- **Pre-registered RD and RKD in administrative data.** The schedule supplies the designs by construction: sharp frontiers at the endpoints ($42,980 / $64,471 / $85,961) and kinks at the pivots. By my count on the SIPP weights, roughly 11.8M workers sit within ±$5,000 of an eligibility endpoint and ~11.2M within ±$5,000 of a pivot; in IRS data the analysis population is effectively the universe, so power is not the constraint — pre-specification is. A manipulation (density) test on prior-year MAGI at the endpoints must be in the protocol, since D7 makes prior-year MAGI the assignment variable and Ramnath documents bunching in exactly this program family.
- **Power and the decision rule.** With cohort arms in the hundreds of thousands to low millions, minimum detectable effects on participation are fractions of a percentage point — far smaller than any policy-relevant response. The decision rule should therefore be stated in cost-effectiveness units, e.g.: if the incremental cost per marginal participant of 200-versus-100 exceeds a named ceiling (the default-only benchmark of ~$304 is the natural reference point), the floor de-escalates.
- **Who runs it.** Treasury OTA with IRS/SOI access for the data; an outside, independently funded research consortium under a §6103-style agreement for the analysis; AEA-registry pre-registration; statutory reporting deadlines to JCT. The evaluator must not be the program administrator.
- **One scope tension to resolve in drafting:** D9 pushes phase-in to policymakers, but randomization and phased rollout are the same object. S2 must reserve the randomization authority in statute or the evaluation dies in implementation.

## 3. Fresh eyes

**Federal account (Q2).** Keep it. From the burden-and-evaluation lens D2 is correct: a single Treasury rail is what makes the auto-accepted deposit (D10), the randomized arm, and population-scale measurement possible. The marketplace alternative reproduces the information-remedy fallacy I documented in round one for TrumpIRA.gov.

**Anchor population (Q3).** The ⅔-of-median calibration is openly reverse-engineered to reproduce coverage on a filer anchor for a worker universe; that is acceptable, but the filer and worker medians can drift apart across decades. Publish the population-gap decomposition at every decennial re-anchor so the "two-thirds" handle stays honest.

**80 percent as central (Q4).** As stated it imports new-hire payroll stickiness onto a branch that includes 4.26M self-employed eligibles with no payroll. My advice on the open Tier 2.1/2.13 values: a W-2 payroll-default rate in the high-70s to mid-80s is defensible (Madrian–Shea 86 percent is the upper anchor; the SIPP-observed 59 percent the floor); the self-employed rate is an opt-in-like margin and should be priced far lower — the experimental opt-in record tops out at 14 percent, and even generous credit for the D10 auto-debit rails puts it well under half the W-2 rate. A rail-weighted central case would land below 80 percent; until those rungs are published, $19.09B should be labeled an assumption, not an estimate. On Tier 2.2, the state auto-IRA persistence anchor (≈0.59) is plausibly conservative for a *matched* account — make persistence a pre-registered S2 outcome rather than an assumption.

**D6 six-month forfeiture (Tier 2.3 advisory).** The cost overstatement is bounded and small: if x percent of matched contributions are withdrawn within six months, the headline falls by roughly x percent of match outlays — even a 10 percent propensity is about $1.4B on $14.15B. Bound it with early-withdrawal flow rates from low-balance Roth-type accounts halved to a six-month window; do not over-engineer it. Two design notes: the forfeiture cliff at month six will produce withdrawal bunching at month seven — a free diagnostic of how binding the rule is — and the Roth-liquid-but-match-forfeitable structure is a comprehension trap for exactly this population, so the D7 legibility layer must state the forfeiture rule, not just the match promise.

**D7 prior-year basis.** Beyond dissolving the reconciliation problem, it creates useful identifying variation: workers with identical current income but different lagged income face different match rates, supporting a clean salience/response test. The new-entrant edge case is an equity decision, not a footnote — first filers skew young and low-income, the target population; default them to the most generous band pending the first return.

**Debt financing (Q5).** Outside my lane except for one point: the RAND offset is directional and decades-out. S2's measured intermediate outcomes (balances at the bottom of the distribution) are what would eventually make the offset claim testable; that is another reason to legislate it.

## 4. Updated verdict

**Support with modifications — unchanged from round one, but closer to support.** D10 fully resolved my delivery objection; D2 and D7 strengthen the design's evaluability; D6 is a sensible liquidity trade. The remaining gap is exactly one item. Conditions, in priority order:

1. **Legislate S2 with the content above** — randomized 50/100/200 arm, pre-registered RD/RKD with a density test, independent evaluator, statutory deadlines, and a pre-specified de-escalation default for the 200 percent floor. With that, I accept D3; without it, my staging recommendation stands.
2. **Publish rail-specific take-up rungs (Tier 2.1/2.13)** before the $19.09B figure is presented as central.
3. **Extend the legibility layer to the six-month forfeiture rule** and adopt the most-generous-band default for first filers.
