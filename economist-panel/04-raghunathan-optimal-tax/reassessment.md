## 2. Does the D7 prior-year basis mute the EMTR concern, or time-shift it?

Both, and the distinction should be the framing of Tier 2.11. Mechanically, prior-year keying does **not** remove the wedge for a forward-looking worker: an extra dollar earned this year raises next year's determination base and reduces next year's match by exactly the adder, so the present-value wedge is δ × adder — about 95 percent of the contemporaneous wedge for a steady earner at any sensible discount rate. The phaseout becomes a one-year-lagged income tax, not no tax. What D7 *genuinely* dissolves is the reconciliation/clawback exposure, the within-year uncertainty, and — most importantly — the salience channel: the rate is a known, already-determined parameter at the moment of contribution, and the empirical labor-supply literature on low-salience, lagged tax parameters consistently finds responses far below the mechanical wedge. So "muted" is defensible as a *behavioral* claim and wrong as a *mechanical* one. My advice for Tier 2.11: compute the stacked match + EITC + CTC + payroll profile on the prior-year basis and report two bounds — the full-salience PV wedge (δ × adder stacked on the contemporaneous programs) and a salience-discounted case — concentrated where it binds. On my refreshed numbers that is 5.80 million exposed workers within $5,000 of their endpoint at a mean adder of 4.95 pp, sitting on top of the 15.98–21.06 percent EITC phaseout and the 7.65 percent payroll tax.

One new D7 asymmetry worth a sentence in the spec: a worker whose income *falls* faces last year's higher-income, less generous rate in exactly the year she is poorer. A one-way current-year election (use current-year MAGI when it is lower, reconciled at filing) would fix the timing for income drops at the cost of reintroducing reconciliation for electors only; the no-prior-return edge case needs the same rule anyway.

**Tier 2.9 / D4 (FPL from prior-year MAGI).** Advise, not flag: the graduated 1/2/3 percent default creates steps at the 100/150 percent FPL boundaries, but these are notches in the *default path*, not in the budget set — the contribution choice itself remains continuous and the match schedule has no brackets. A prior-year-MAGI-to-FPL approximation with a default household size is acceptable; just say explicitly that the boundary discontinuity lives in the default architecture, where stickiness rather than incentives governs, and verify the implied match-dollar step (rate × 1 pp of earnings, roughly $150–$200 near 100 percent FPL for a Single filer) in the costed option.

## 3. Fresh eyes

**The anchor population (focus question 3 — my lane).** Be honest about what the two-thirds rule is: a compensating constant. The chosen pivot, 0.8 × the IRS filer median ($40,294 TY2027), equals $32,235 — which is 0.588 × the SIPP *worker* median ($54,799), i.e., the old 0.6×-worker-median geometry recovered to within two percent. The calibration was reverse-engineered to preserve coverage (44.47M vs. 46.07M), and at the base year that is fine; the gap decomposition in `_shared/anchoring/FINDINGS.md` correctly shows the filer-vs-worker gap is population, not concept. The risk is at re-anchor time: the worker/filer median ratio (~1.36) is not a constant of nature. The all-single-filer median moves with filing composition — filing-threshold changes, credit-induced filing, retiree and gig-filer shares. Worse, there is an **endogeneity loop unique to this anchor**: a refundable match that successfully induces low-income non-filers to file will mechanically *lower* the single-filer median AGI and drag its own eligibility frontier down at the next decennial re-anchor. The program's success erodes its own coverage. Per decade the magnitude is plausibly modest, but the sign is perverse. Two repairs, either sufficient: (i) define the anchor population as single returns *with positive wage or self-employment income* (still IRS-published, far closer to the scored worker universe, immune to retiree/credit-filer composition), re-tuning the fraction to whatever reproduces the same base-year pivot; or (ii) keep the all-filer median but legislate a **re-anchor corridor** (cap each decennial jump at, say, ±10 percent) so a composition shock cannot move millions across the frontier in one step. I prefer (i) with (ii) as a belt. I also note for distributional honesty that the Single endpoint ($42,980) now sits $11,818 *below* the SIPP single-worker median — the median single worker is ineligible by design; the spec should say so plainly.

**Geometry on the new pivots.** The re-anchor mildly *worsened* the schedule geometry I conditionally praised: slopes steepen to 4.65 / 3.10 / 2.33 pp per $1,000 (Single/HoH/MFJ, from 4.56/3.04/2.28), the zero-EMTR cap plateau shrinks to 5.39M workers (12.1 percent of eligible, from 12.6), and 39.08M workers carrying $1.18 trillion of earnings sit on the sloped segment at a mean adder of 3.26 pp. The endpoint adder remains the design invariant 6.0 pp; the MFJ diagonal cap window is now $22,618–$63,343 of joint MAGI, and the cap still never binds for Single ($645 peak) or HoH ($967 peak) at the 3 percent default. None of this is disqualifying — it is the same trade at marginally steeper prices — but the brief should not recycle plateau rhetoric computed on the old pivots.

**The 200 percent floor (FQ1).** D3's rationale concedes my round-one point: the model assumes match-invariant participation, so its "no new savers" result cannot adjudicate the floor. The H&R Block evidence (take-up 3→8→14 percent at 0/20/50 percent matches) says participation responds to the match price but modestly, and 200 percent is far out of sample. Add the geometric cost: with the pivot fixed at 50 percent, the 200 percent floor is what makes the slope 150/pivot — a 100 percent floor would carry one-third the wedge. The floor's price is not only dollars; it is slope. I will support keeping it **only with the S2 evaluation in the statute** (randomized 50/100/200 arms); that is the correct resolution mechanism for a bet the model structurally cannot score.

**Federal account (FQ2).** From my vantage the channel choice is zero-EMTR — it moves the availability of the budget set, not its slope — and worker-level portability protects accumulation against churn. I support D2 and defer the coalition arithmetic to colleagues whose lane it is.

**80 percent as central (FQ4).** Acceptable presentation of an identical ladder, provided the $14.15B–$23.87B band stays visible and the uniform 80 percent is conditioned by rail: applying auto-enrollment stickiness to the 4.4M self-employed eligibles, who have no payroll, is the weak link (Tier 2.1/2.13 should discipline it).

**Debt financing (FQ5).** The RAND offset is correctly labeled directional; keep it that way. My EMTR-adjacent caution: if the eventual pay-for is itself a phaseout-bearing instrument, the stacking analysis worsens. Okafor's DC-exclusion envelope remains the progressivity-consistent pay-for; for a $14–19B program I do not treat its absence as disqualifying.

## 4. Updated verdict: **Support** (moved up from support with modifications)

Two of my three round-one recommendations are adopted and verified (the anchor, the marriage prose); the third is open with a reframing I largely endorse. The schedule remains the best-targeted geometry on the table — marriage-neutral at the symmetric benchmark, bounded windfall, frontier now legislatable. Conditions, in order:

1. **Fix the anchor-population endogeneity before drafting**: anchor on single filers with positive earned income (or add a decennial re-anchor corridor), so the frontier cannot be moved by filing-composition shocks the program itself induces.
2. **Complete Tier 2.11 in the dual framing** — PV wedge vs. salience-discounted — on the prior-year basis, before external release; "muted by D7" is a behavioral claim and should be presented as one, not as the disappearance of the wedge.
3. **Legislate the S2 evaluation as the price of the 200 percent floor.**
