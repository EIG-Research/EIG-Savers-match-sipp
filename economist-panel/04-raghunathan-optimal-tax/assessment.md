**Panelist:** Dr. Priya Raghunathan (public economics; optimal taxation, Saez/Mirrlees tradition)
**Date:** 2026-06-10
**Subject:** Universal Account, Targeted Saver's Match hybrid (`Infrastructure/specs/2026-05-28_universal-account-savers-match-hybrid-proposal.md`)

## Vantage point

I evaluate transfer programs by the geometry of the budget sets they create: the effective marginal tax rates (EMTRs) they impose through phaseouts, how those wedges stack on the rest of the tax-transfer system, and whether the schedule treats equals equally — particularly across marital arrangements. A means-tested retirement match is a price subsidy on saving whose income-targeting converts it into an implicit income tax over the phaseout range; the design question is where that implicit tax lands, on whom, and at what rate. Every quantitative claim below is computed from the repository's pivot table and row-level simulation universe (`economist-panel/04-raghunathan-optimal-tax/code/`).

## 1. The implicit EMTR profile of the redesigned schedule

The hybrid match rate is a single line per filing group: 200 percent at zero MAGI, falling at 150/pivot percentage points per dollar — 4.56, 3.04, and 2.28 pp per $1,000 of MAGI for Single ($32,879 pivot), Head of Household ($49,319), and MFJ ($65,758), hitting zero at $43,839 / $65,758 / $87,678 (`data/processed/universal_sm_hybrid/pivot_table.parquet`). For a worker contributing the 3 percent default, the benefit is min(rate × 0.03 × earnings, $1,000), so the phase-out EMTR adder — the loss of match per extra dollar of MAGI, contribution held fixed — is 150 × contribution / pivot wherever the cap does not bind, and exactly **zero** wherever it does. My profile along the earnings-equals-MAGI diagonal (`figures/fig1_emtr_profile_by_filing_group.png`; `tables/emtr_profile_summary.csv`) shows:

- The adder rises linearly from zero at zero MAGI to about 4.5 pp at the pivot and **6.0 pp at the endpoint for every filing group** (an exact design invariant: 150 × 0.03 × (4/3 × pivot)/pivot = 6 pp).
- The celebrated zero-EMTR cap plateau is real but **narrowly distributed**. On the diagonal at the 3 percent default, a Single filer's unbounded match peaks at $658 (at $22,000 MAGI) and a HoH filer's at $986 — the $1,000 cap *never* binds for them. Only the MFJ single-earner case enjoys the plateau, from $22,500 to $65,250 of joint MAGI. The proposal's own phaseout-lens diagnostic concedes the default reaches the cap "only near the middle of the joint-filer band."
- Against current law: §6433 phases 50 percent to zero over a $15,000 window (Single), so a $2,000 contributor faces a 6.67 pp adder concentrated in $20,500–$35,500 (4.44 pp HoH, 3.33 pp MFJ; IRS, "Notice 2024-65," 2024, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-65.pdf.). The hybrid spreads a *smaller peak* (6.0 pp) over a *much wider band* (all of $0–$43,839 for Single). Honesty requires noting that in rate terms the hybrid is actually steeper per dollar — 4.56 vs. 3.33 pp per $1,000 for Single — and a $2,000 contributor under the hybrid would face up to a 9.1 pp adder; the smoothness advantage materializes only because the 3 percent default implies small contributions at low earnings. The low EMTR is bought by the low default, not by a flat schedule.

## 2. Who actually faces the wedge

Using `simulation_results.parquet` with WPFINWGT weights (`code/03_emtr_exposure.R`; `tables/emtr_exposure_distribution.csv`; `figures/fig2_emtr_exposure_distribution.png`), of the 46.07 million eligible workers:

| Exposure group | Workers (M) | Share of eligible | Earnings ($B) | Mean adder (pp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cap binds — zero implicit EMTR | 5.79 | 12.6 percent | 273.7 | 0 |
| Cap not binding — positive EMTR | 40.28 | 87.4 percent | 1,233.1 | 3.25 |

So **seven of every eight eligible workers, carrying $1.23 trillion of earnings, sit on the sloped part of the schedule**; the zero-EMTR virtue covers 5.79 million workers, three-quarters of them MFJ (3.91M of 15.23M MFJ eligibles are capped, versus only 5.8 percent of Singles and 7.1 percent of HoH). Mean adders among the exposed are 3.71 pp (Single), 3.27 pp (HoH), and 2.35 pp (MFJ), with a Single 90th percentile of 5.6 pp. About 5.86 million workers sit within $5,000 below their endpoint, facing a mean 4.85 pp adder. Two prose caveats: (i) these are mechanical adders conditional on contributing the default — a worker who opts out faces none of it; (ii) for MFJ couples the joint-MAGI keying means both spouses' adders stack on the same marginal household dollar — two equal earners at the joint endpoint face roughly 3.0 pp each, 6.0 pp combined.

**Stacking.** These adders land on workers already facing the system's worst cumulative wedges. CBO estimates low- and moderate-income workers faced an average EMTR of about 31 percent in 2016, with the median rising to roughly 34 percent for earners between 100 and 149 percent of the poverty level (Congressional Budget Office, "Effective Marginal Tax Rates for Low- and Moderate-Income Workers in 2016," 2015, https://www.cbo.gov/publication/50923.). Kosar and Moffitt document that cumulative marginal rates for families combining the federal income tax, payroll tax, and multiple transfer programs are substantially higher than any single-program calculation suggests, concentrated precisely in the EITC phaseout region (Gizem Kosar and Robert A. Moffitt, "Trends in Cumulative Marginal Tax Rates Facing Low-Income Families, 1997–2007," NBER Working Paper 22782, 2016, https://www.nber.org/papers/w22782.). The hybrid's positive-adder band ($0 to $43,839 Single; to $65,758 HoH) overlaps the EITC phaseout (15.98–21.06 percent reduction rates for parents) and the 7.65 percent employee payroll tax along its entire length; CRS documents how exactly this kind of credit phaseout pushes effective rates well above statutory rates (Congressional Research Service, "Statutory, Average, and Effective Marginal Tax Rates in the Federal Individual Income Tax: Background and Analysis," R44787, 2021, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R44787/R44787.4.pdf.). A 3–6 pp adder on top of a 30–50 percent stack is not disqualifying — it is comparable to current law's 6.67 pp over a narrower band — but the repo's own deferred "Framework B" stacked-EMTR analysis (`Infrastructure/plans/2026-05-31_emtr-profile-universal-sm-hybrid.md`) should be completed before enactment, not after.

## 3. Marriage treatment

I built archetype couples under the actual rules — rate keyed to joint MAGI on the MFJ schedule, contributions of 3 percent of personal earnings, $1,000 per-individual cap — and compared each couple with the same two people cohabiting and filing single (`code/02_marriage_archetypes.R`; `tables/marriage_archetypes.csv`):

| Couple (earnings) | Joint MAGI | MFJ rate | Married match | Cohabiting match | Marriage delta | §6433 delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equal, $20k + $20k | $40,000 | 109 percent | $1,305 | $1,305 | $0 | $0 |
| Equal, $35k + $35k | $70,000 | 40 percent | $847 | $847 | $0 | $0 |
| Unequal, $30k + $10k | $40,000 | 109 percent | $1,305 | $1,031 | +$274 | +$285 |
| Unequal, $34k + $10k | $44,000 | 100 percent | $1,299 | $921 | +$378 | +$393 |
| Single earner, $40k + $0 | $40,000 | 109 percent | $1,000 | $210 | +$790 | +$600 |
| Single earner, $60k + $0 | $60,000 | 63 percent | $1,000 | $0 | +$1,000 | +$330 |

Because the MFJ pivot is exactly 2.0 × the Single pivot, rate_MFJ(2x) = rate_Single(x): **equal-earner couples are perfectly marriage-neutral**, and since the cap never clips a single filer at the 3 percent default, **no marriage penalty exists anywhere on the default schedule**. The bonus grows monotonically with earnings asymmetry, peaking at $1,000 for a single-earner couple whose earner would be ineligible as a single filer. This is a defensible structure — marriage-neutral at the symmetric benchmark, subsidizing single-earner couples as §6433 does (my §6433 columns show identical signs) — and the proposal's claim that it "mirrors §6433/Form 8880" is verified. But the proposal's own prose has the signs backwards relative to the cohabiting benchmark: it asserts a "marriage *bonus*" for dual-earner couples (my computation: exactly zero for equal earners) and a "relative penalty" for single-earner couples (my computation: the largest bonus in the table; the penalty is only relative to dual-earner couples with the same joint MAGI, who can collect two caps). The drafting should be corrected before external release. The real equity cost is the classic secondary-earner distortion: the non-earning spouse in the $40,000 couple who takes a $20,000 job earns a gross match of $379 on her own contribution but claws back $243 of the primary earner's previously capped match — a net household gain of $136, against the $653 the same worker would collect unmarried. Joint-MAGI keying taxes second earners here exactly as it does in the EITC.

## 4. Design judgments from the optimal-tax vantage

**The 200 percent floor.** Saez's optimal-transfer analysis shows that when labor-supply responses are concentrated on the extensive margin — as they are for low earners — optimal programs subsidize participation at the bottom and phase out gradually, rather than guaranteeing income with steep clawbacks (Emmanuel Saez, "Optimal Income Transfer Programs: Intensive versus Extensive Labor Supply Responses," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 117(3), 2002, https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/botqje.pdf.). An analogous logic applies to the saving margin: the H&R Block experiment found match rates of 0/20/50 percent moved IRA take-up from 3 to 8 to 14 percent — participation responds to the match price, though not explosively (Esther Duflo, William Gale, Jeffrey Liebman, Peter Orszag, and Emmanuel Saez, "Saving Incentives for Low- and Middle-Income Families: Evidence from a Field Experiment with H&R Block," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 121(4), 2006, https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/duflo-gale-liebman-orszag-saezQJE06block_small.pdf.). The 200 percent rate at zero MAGI is therefore best read not as inverse-elasticity targeting (we have no evidence the price elasticity of saving is four times higher at $5,000 than at $33,000) but as a transparent way to deliver a near-demogrant: with auto-enrollment doing the behavioral work, the high rate at the bottom is mostly an infra-marginal transfer — a windfall — to default-contributing workers. I do not consider that damning: paired with the universal account, it is a progressive wealth-building grant with a work requirement, and the $1,000 cap bounds the windfall per person. But the proposal should own that framing rather than implying a price-response rationale.

**Data-anchored pivot indexing.** Re-anchoring pivots to each SIPP wave is the proposal's weakest design feature. Statutory parameters should be predictable, contestable, and replicable; a pivot equal to 0.6 × the weighted-median MAGI of a survey-defined worker universe is none of these. Survey redesigns, sampling error (no replicate-weight intervals yet, by the repo's own admission), and the SIPP-vs-§6433 MAGI divergence (flagged "material" in the legal review) would all move millions of workers across the eligibility frontier with no congressional vote. Median-indexing has real merit — it keeps targeting constant in relative-income space, unlike §6433's CPI-from-static-2027-thresholds — but the anchor should be an administrative series (the SSA average wage index, or tax-record medians), set in statute with the SIPP used only for design calibration. RSAA itself indexes to "median personal income," showing the drafting is feasible (S.1526, 119th Congress, https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1526.).

**Versus RSAA's geometry.** RSAA keys the match to the contribution *rate* — 100 percent on the first 3 percent of income, 50 percent on the next 2, plus a 1 percent automatic contribution, with the credit limit falling $75 per $1,000 of income above median-income thresholds (S.1526; `economist-panel/_shared/sources/rsaa-s1526-119th-bill-text.pdf`). Three observations. First, a rate-keyed match is proportional to income for any fixed contribution rate, so RSAA delivers more federal dollars to a $60,000 worker than a $20,000 worker contributing the same share — the hybrid's MAGI-keyed schedule with a flat $1,000 cap is unambiguously better targeted toward the bottom. Second, RSAA's kinks at 3 and 5 percent invite bunching at the kink points, while its $75-per-$1,000 limit-reduction creates its own implicit EMTR of up to 7.5 percent of the credit limit — geometry no smoother than the hybrid's. Third, RSAA's 1 percent automatic contribution is the one element I would steal: it is a pure zero-EMTR demogrant, immune to every wedge computed above. On phaseout geometry and targeting, the hybrid wins; on the existence of an unconditional floor contribution, RSAA wins.

## 5. Political economy: §6433, the EO, and where this proposal fits

The Saver's Match passed because it was the rare refundable expansion wrapped in bipartisan pension legislation — SECURE 2.0 (Division T of P.L. 117-328), built from the Cardin–Portman lineage and near-unanimous committee products (CRS, "The Saver's Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). It fixed the §25B credit's nonrefundability — a credit only 5.7 percent of taxpayers claimed, averaging $191 (Mark J. Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.) — but left the access gap: the match must land in an account the worker already has, plans are not required to accept it, and as of June 2026 Treasury has issued no proposed regulations beyond Notice 2024-65, with IRSAC warning that the program's success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions" (ASPPA, "Prioritize the Saver's Match, Advisory Council Tells IRS," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.).

The April 30, 2026 executive order and TrumpIRA.gov attack the deposit-destination problem from the IRA side — a Treasury-run comparison portal of sub-0.15 percent-fee, no-minimum IRAs that accept §6433(e)(2)(C) deposits by January 1, 2027 (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.). From my vantage the EO is a **zero-EMTR intervention**: it changes the availability of the budget set, not its slope or its generosity — match rate, $2,000 base, and phaseout thresholds are all statutory and untouched. That is its virtue (no new wedges) and its limit (no new money, no defaults, no enrollment). It validates this proposal's diagnosis while leaving the entire parameter space — where this proposal lives — to Congress, as the EO's own Section 6 legislative-recommendations directive concedes.

The hybrid is, in effect, the statutory bid the EO invites: the account (universal default), the contribution (3 percent auto-enrollment), and a schedule concentrating 82.8 percent of dollars in the bottom three universe deciles at a $14.9 billion headline cost. It inherits RSAA's unresolved liabilities — employer crowd-out (unmodeled here; Morningstar's RSAA simulations show meaningful displacement risk: Spencer Look and Jack VanDerhei, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," Morningstar, 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.), no official score, and industry opposition — but its per-person dollar cap bounds fiscal exposure in a way RSAA's percent-of-payroll structure does not.

## Verdict: **Support with modifications**

The schedule is a genuine improvement on §6433's geometry — a lower peak wedge spread thinly, perfect marriage neutrality for equal earners, a bounded and well-targeted windfall — and the universal account fixes the flaw that makes current law underperform. But the design's zero-EMTR selling point covers only 12.6 percent of eligible workers, the adder lands on 40.3 million workers already in the EITC stacking zone, and the SIPP-anchored pivot is not legislatable as drafted.

**Top three recommendations:**

1. **Replace SIPP-wave re-anchoring with a statutory anchor on an administrative series** (SSA average wage index or IRS tax-record median), with SIPP relegated to calibration. Predictability and replicability of the eligibility frontier are non-negotiable for a parameter that moves millions of workers.
2. **Complete the stacked-EMTR analysis (the deferred Framework B) before external release, and report the combined match + EITC + payroll wedge by filing group.** If the combined wedge near the HoH and Single endpoints is unacceptable, widen the cap-binding plateau — for example, a floor rate or minimum-match region near the endpoint — rather than steepening the slope.
3. **Correct the marriage-treatment prose and address the secondary-earner margin.** State that the schedule is marriage-neutral for equal earners and bonus-generating for asymmetric couples (verified here against cohabiting singles), and consider a secondary-earner MAGI deduction analogous to proposals in the EITC literature, since joint-MAGI keying otherwise imports the standard second-earner distortion. Adopting RSAA's 1 percent automatic contribution as a zero-EMTR floor is a complementary improvement worth costing.
