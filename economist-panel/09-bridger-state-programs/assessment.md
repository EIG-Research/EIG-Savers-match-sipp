# Panel Assessment 09 — The Universal Account Through the State Auto-IRA Lens

**Panelist:** Dr. Tom Bridger
**Date:** 2026-06-11
**Verdict:** Support with modifications

## Vantage point

I evaluate state automatic-enrollment IRA programs — OregonSaves, CalSavers, Illinois Secure Choice — using their administrative dashboards, enforcement records, and the academic literature built on their account-level data. These programs are the only at-scale field test of the exact mechanism this proposal would federalize: auto-enrollment of workers without employer plans into portable, payroll-deducted IRAs. My question for the hybrid proposal is simple: when its universal-account route is priced with parameters the states have actually observed — rather than assumed — what happens to coverage, contributions, and the $14.9 billion headline?

## What the state evidence says

Nine years in, the state programs are real but modest: $3.03 billion in assets across more than 1.2 million funded accounts as of April 2026, with 22 programs enacted and 17 operational (Emily Boyle, PLANSPONSOR, 2026, "State-Facilitated Retirement Savings Assets Hit $3B," https://www.plansponsor.com/state-facilitated-retirement-savings-assets-hit-3b/.). The parameters that matter for calibration, all in `tables/state_program_parameters.csv`:

- **Participation.** CalSavers' effective opt-out rate is 34.82 percent (CalSavers Retirement Savings Board, 2025, "Participation & Funding Snapshot as of 8/31/2025," https://www.treasurer.ca.gov/calsavers/reports/2025/august_2025.pdf.); OregonSaves' 30-day opt-out rate is 27.0 percent (Oregon Treasury Savings Network, 2025, "OregonSaves 2024 Annual Report," https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/citizen_engagement/Reports/OregonRetirementSavingsBoard2025.pdf.). But opt-out understates non-participation: the definitive OregonSaves study finds a *feasible* participation rate of 62.4 percent and a *global* funded-account rate of just 34.3 percent, with 40.9 percent formally opting out within three months (John Chalmers, Olivia S. Mitchell, Jonathan Reuter, and Mingli Zhong, 2021, "Auto-Enrollment Retirement Plans in OregonSaves," MRDRC Working Paper 2021-425, https://mrdrc.isr.umich.edu/publications/papers/pdf/wp425.pdf.). Quinby and coauthors bound positive-balance participation at 48 to 67 percent among workers at contributing employers (cited in Chalmers et al., 2021, footnote 12.).
- **Contributions.** Conditional on contributing in the past 30 days, the median CalSavers saver puts in $148 per month ($194 mean) at a 5.23 percent average rate; OregonSaves medians are $141 ($185 mean) at a 6.6 percent average rate. Both programs default at 5 percent with automatic one-point annual escalation — and savers overwhelmingly accept it: 93.3 percent of non-opt-outs sit exactly at the default, and escalation accounts for nearly all rate increases (Chalmers et al., 2021, Tables 7-8.).
- **Persistence.** The deep problem is churn, not the level. Covered firms have 38.2 percent annual turnover; the probability of twelve consecutive monthly contributions is 30 percent; and the share of open accounts receiving any inflow averages 59.4 percent over the first year (my computation from Chalmers et al., 2021, Table 11, in `code/01_state_calibration.R`).
- **Employer compliance.** CalSavers launched statewide in July 2019; the waves whose deadlines passed three or more years ago have an 85.3 percent employer response rate, but the all-wave rate including the 1-4-employee cohort is 34.9 percent, and only 60,931 of roughly 332,000 post-deadline employers — 18 percent — have reached full payroll facilitation (CalSavers Snapshot, 8/31/2025.). Penalty referrals to the Franchise Tax Board ($250, then $500 per eligible employee) began only in October 2023, four years after launch; of 9,949 Wave-2023 penalty notices mailed in late 2024, about 24 percent of recipients complied (CalSavers Retirement Savings Board, 2025, "Executive Director's Report," March 6, 2025, https://www.treasurer.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2025-12/3_7.pdf.). The Wave 4 deadline did move registrations — from 255,793 employers on December 31, 2025 to 277,902 by February 28, 2026 (NAPA, 2026, "State Auto-IRA Programs Show No Signs of Slowing Down," https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/3/state-auto-ira-programs-show-no-signs-of-slowing-down/.) — but facilitation lags registration by years.

## Re-pricing the universal-account route (Figure 1, `tables/state_calibrated_scenarios.csv`)

The repo's headline applies observed row-level participation to the 15.99 million eligible workers with an existing DC account and a uniform 59.8 percent rate to the remaining 30.07 million; my code reproduces the published figure to within $1 million ($14.945 billion). I then re-price the 22.01 million universal-account workers *without* an existing DC account — precisely the population state auto-IRAs serve, since only about 10 percent of OregonSaves-targeted workers had their own plan (Chalmers et al., 2021.) — holding everything else at the repo treatment.

|---|---|---|---|

| State upper: 62.4 percent feasible participation | 28.1 | $15.3B | 102 percent |

| State lower: 34.3 percent global participation | 21.9 | $11.9B | 79 percent |
| Contribution persistence (59.4 percent of default dollars arrive) | 27.5 | $12.3B | 82 percent |
| Contributions capped at state median dollars ($1,019/yr) | 27.5 | $14.5B | 97 percent |
| **State-evidence-calibrated central (48 percent × persistence)** | **25.0** | **$11.4B** | **76 percent** |
| State-evidence-calibrated lower (34.3 percent × persistence × cap) | 21.9 | $10.1B | 68 percent |

Three findings. First, the repo's 59.8 percent assumption sits *inside* the state evidence band — essentially at the optimistic end (62.4 percent feasible). That is a point in the proposal's favor: the headline is not fantasy. Second, the binding state lesson is persistence, not the contribution level. Multiplying default dollars by the OregonSaves first-year inflow factor (0.594) removes $2.7 billion by itself. Third, my central state-calibrated estimate is **$11.4 billion — 76 percent of the headline survives** — with a band of $10.1 billion to $15.3 billion. The cheaper program is not better: the savings come from roughly 2.6 to 5.6 million fewer participating workers and from match dollars that never get earned because contributions stop when jobs end.

## Contribution adequacy (Figure 2)

A counterintuitive result: observed state contribution *dollars* are not the problem for this design. The eligible universal-account population is so low-paid that the proposal's 3 percent default implies a mean contribution of only $72 per month (weighted median $821 per year). Rescaled to a 3 percent default, the CalSavers median contributor's $148 per month becomes $85 — *above* the proposal's median assumption. Capping contributions at rate-adjusted state median dollars therefore trims the cost only to $14.5 billion (97 percent). What state savers do not do is contribute *continuously*: 38 percent annual turnover and a 30 percent twelve-consecutive-month probability mean the match-qualifying flow is roughly 60 percent of the annualized default. Because the hybrid's match rate reaches 200 percent at the bottom, partial-year contributors still capture meaningful match dollars — the linear schedule is genuinely well-suited to intermittent savers — but the headline should be quoted with persistence built in.

## Employer-mandate reality (Figure 3, `tables/compliance_ramp_scenario.csv`)

CalSavers needed three deadlines (2020-2022), a statutory expansion, and tax-authority penalty enforcement starting in year five to reach 85.3 percent employer response in its mature waves — and even Oregon, nine years in, shows only 8,293 of 31,723 registered employers actively submitting payroll in the trailing three months (OregonSaves 2024 Annual Report.). Pew's employer survey is reassuring on burden — roughly 80 percent of participating Oregon employers report no out-of-pocket cost (John Scott and Mark Hines, 2020, "Employers Express Satisfaction With New Oregon Retirement Savings Program," The Pew Charitable Trusts, https://www.pewtrusts.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2020/07/30/employers-express-satisfaction-with-new-oregon-retirement-savings-program.) — but low burden did not produce fast compliance. Applying a CalSavers-anchored coverage ramp (30/55/75 percent of W-2 universal-route coverage in years one through three, maturing at 85.3 percent; year-1-to-3 shares are labeled scenario assumptions) to my central calibration yields **$9.1 billion in year one, $9.9 billion in year two, $10.6 billion in year three, and $10.9 billion at maturity**. A federal mandate could ramp faster — the IRS sees every employer through payroll tax filings, unlike states assembling registries from scratch — but only if enforcement is funded and automatic from day one, which took California four years. The 14.4 percent of my calibration base that is self-employed has no payroll mechanism at all; the repo itself flags this, and state programs offer no evidence either way because self-enrollment is trivial there (1,186 self-enrolled funded accounts in Oregon).

## What the states cannot tell us — and what they can

**The match is the genuinely new ingredient.** Every state number above comes from *match-free* programs. The single most common opt-out reason is "I can't afford to save at this time" (38.6 percent of stated reasons), concentrated in low-wage industries (Chalmers et al., 2021, Table 4.). A 100-to-200 percent federal match is a direct price subsidy aimed exactly at that margin; no state benchmark contains it. This is the strongest reason to expect participation *above* the 48 percent central calibration — and why I treat the repo's 59.8 percent as defensible rather than optimistic. The offsetting worry is salience: the match arrives through tax filing the following year, not in the paycheck where the opt-out decision lives. Design matters more than the rate here.

**Leakage will erode the policy's stock objective.** CalSavers has paid out $447 million in withdrawals against $1.6 billion contributed (28 percent), and 24.6 percent of payroll-contributing accounts have taken a full withdrawal; OregonSaves' cumulative withdrawals are 36 percent of contributions, and 45 percent of 2024 contributions flowed out the same year (program reports above.). Withdrawals spike at job separation — these accounts function partly as emergency buffers (Chalmers et al., 2021.). For the hybrid, leakage cuts both ways: §6433(d)(2)'s distribution recapture (unmodeled in the repo, as disclosed) would claw back match eligibility for withdrawers, lowering cost but also gutting accumulation for exactly the high-churn workers the design targets.

**TrumpIRA.gov.** The April 30, 2026 executive order is a listing-and-comparison portal with fee caps — no accounts, no auto-enrollment, no default (economist-panel/_shared/policy-context.md.). State evidence is blunt about this: before auto-enrollment, only 22.1 percent of workers at plan-less firms had ever opened an IRA and 7.6 percent contributed actively (Chalmers et al., 2021, citing 2014 SIPP.), and OregonSaves' core finding is that *search costs*, not information, were the barrier auto-enrollment removed. A portal that still requires the worker to search, choose, and enroll fixes the deposit-destination problem the IRSAC flagged, but state evidence predicts it moves participation by single digits at best. The hybrid's automatic architecture is the part the EO cannot do and the part the evidence supports.

**Coexistence with 17 operational state programs.** The proposal sensibly lets compliant state auto-IRAs substitute for the federal account. That clause needs real engineering: asset transition or grandfathering for 1.2 million funded accounts, fee standards (state program all-in fees generally exceed the EO's 0.15 percent benchmark), and a rule preventing employers from arbitraging between regimes. The states are also an implementation asset — nine years of registry-building, exemption-processing, and penalty workflow that Treasury should buy, not rebuild.

**Versus the RSAA (S.1526).** From my vantage point the decisive design difference is the default. RSAA's 3 percent auto-enrollment is *below* universal state practice; the hybrid's flat 3 percent with no escalation is too. Oregon and California default at 5 percent and escalate one point per year, and their savers accept it — escalation produced 23,394 observed 5-to-6 percent increases versus trivial voluntary changes (Chalmers et al., 2021, Table 8.). A flat 3 percent default forfeits the cheapest, best-evidenced savings-increasing instrument in the auto-IRA toolkit. The hybrid's MAGI-keyed match is better targeted than RSAA's contribution-rate-keyed match for intermittent low-wage savers (it does not require sustained high contribution rates to earn the top subsidy), and its $1,000 per-person cap bounds fiscal exposure in a way RSAA's 5-percent-of-income structure does not. Marry the hybrid's match geometry to RSAA's (and the states') 5-percent-with-escalation default and the design dominates both parents. The political economy lesson of §6433 — it passed in 2022 precisely because it was a bounded, bipartisan, account-delivered match, and it now stalls on the access gap and on voluntary plan acceptance of deposits (Notice 2024-65; IRSAC 2026, per the shared policy brief.) — argues for exactly this kind of incremental, capped architecture.

## Verdict: Support with modifications

The proposal's mechanism is the one the state experiments validate, its headline participation assumption sits at the optimistic edge of — but inside — the observed band, and its match fills the one hole the match-free state programs could not. My state-calibrated central cost is $11.4 billion (range $10.1-$15.3 billion), with a year-one spend nearer $9 billion under any realistic compliance ramp.

**Top three recommendations:**

1. **Adopt the state default, not a weaker one.** Raise the default contribution to 5 percent of earnings with automatic one-point annual escalation to 10 percent, mirroring OregonSaves/CalSavers practice that savers demonstrably accept; re-score the proposal on that basis.
2. **Score and budget on a compliance ramp with day-one enforcement.** Publish year-by-year cost and coverage paths (my anchors: 30/55/75/85 percent), build penalty enforcement into the IRS payroll-tax pipeline from year one rather than year five, and contract with existing state programs as transition infrastructure with explicit substitution, fee, and account-portability standards.
3. **Engineer for churn and leakage.** Quote the headline with a contribution-persistence adjustment; pair the account with a liquid emergency-savings sublayer (the OregonSaves $1,000 money-market default is a working prototype) so that job-separation withdrawals do not trigger §6433(d)(2) match clawbacks that fall hardest on the highest-churn workers; and add a self-employed contribution mechanism tied to estimated-tax filings, with its own take-up sensitivity.
