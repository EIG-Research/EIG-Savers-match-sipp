## 2. The declines

**D4, the 3 percent default: my objection stands on accumulation, but I withdraw the re-score demand.** The legibility rationale does not answer the evidence — Oregon and California default at 5 percent with one-point annual escalation, 93.3 percent of non-opt-outs sit exactly at the default, and escalation produced essentially all observed rate increases (Chalmers et al. 2021, Tables 7–8). Savers stay in; the question was never whether 3 percent is legible. And the liquidity half of the rationale is now *self-undercut by the author's own D6*: once a worker's contributions are Roth-accessible at will, a too-high default is self-correcting, so the affordability case for a low default is weaker than when I first made the objection. That said, two things changed my posture. First, the $1,000 cap means the fiscal headline barely moves with the default — above the cap-reaching window, a higher default buys unmatched (still valuable) saving at zero federal cost, so this is a design dispute, not a scoring dispute. Second, the cheapest compromise is sitting in plain view: **keep the legible 3 percent headline and add the auto-escalator (one point per year to 7 or 10 percent)**. Escalation, not the starting level, is the best-evidenced instrument in the state toolkit, and D4 discarded both when it only needed to discard one.

**The graduated 1/2/3 percent-by-FPL option is worse than flat 3 percent, on the state evidence — and it contradicts D3.** I priced it on the universal-route calibration base (21.51M workers without an existing DC account): 43.7 percent of them sit below 150 percent of the single-person FPL, and the graduated default cuts their mean full-participation match from $471 to $253 — a 46 percent cut in federal match dollars aimed at precisely the workers the 200 percent floor exists to reach (route full-participation cost falls from $11.7B to $9.65B). The author cannot simultaneously bet (D3) that over-matching the bottom buys participation the model cannot see and carry an option that halves the bottom's matched dollars. D6 already does the liquidity-accommodation job without touching the default; the FPL band also needs household size a payroll file does not carry. Drop the option, or recast it as an opt-down floor rather than a default.

**D9: I accept the steady-state scope, with one flag.** A design document is entitled to score the destination, and my ramp anchors (CalSavers needed four years and FTB enforcement to reach 85.3 percent response in mature waves) are transition facts. But the ten-year path (`_shared/anchoring/ten_year_path.md`) quotes **FY2027–FY2036 outlays of ~$216B from a $19.09B FY2027 base** — that is not steady-state-only; it is a fiscal path that implicitly assumes full employer facilitation in year one, which no operating program has achieved. My first-round ramp cut year one by roughly 17 percent relative to maturity. One sentence in the path note ("the FY2027–28 figures assume instant facilitation; any realistic ramp shifts $5–15B of the ten-year total later or off the table") would reconcile the two documents. The binding year-one constraint is not hidden by the spec; it is hidden by the path memo.

## 3. Fresh eyes — the five focus questions and the open Tier-2 assumptions

**Tier 2.2, persistence — my core advice: use 0.70, not 0.59.** The state factor ≈0.59 is the *wrong anchor* for this design, because it embeds the failure mode the design removes. In Chalmers et al. (2021) Table 11, the first-year inflow share is ~0.594 overall, but decomposes into active employees at 0.687 and *separated* workers collapsing to 7–9 percent — because state contributions are keyed to the originating employer's payroll. A worker-level portable account under a universal employer mandate re-establishes the deduction automatically at the next covered job, so the separated-worker collapse largely disappears; the active-only series (0.687) is the portability counterfactual, and the match plus the six-month vesting hold add a small retention margin on top. **Recommended: 0.70 central, band 0.62–0.75** (0.62 = state-observed plus only a modest portability gain). Applied to the universal route, 0.70 takes the conservative headline from $14.15B to **$12.32B**.

**Tier 2.1, take-up — recommended W-2 rate 65 percent (band 55–75); self-employed 25 percent (band 15–35).** CalSavers' effective opt-out is 34.8 percent (→ ~65 percent in); OregonSaves' feasible participation is 62.4 percent — both *match-free*. A 100–200 percent match aimed at the modal opt-out reason ("can't afford to save," 38.6 percent of stated reasons) plausibly adds a few points to the match-free 62; 65 is my central. The self-employed have no payroll default on any rail D10 names, and pre-auto-enrollment voluntary IRA activity among plan-less workers ran 8–22 percent; a match premium gets you to ~25, not 59. Combining: **my state-evidence central on the current base is $12.41B (87.7 percent of the conservative headline; 26.3M participants), band $11.1–$13.7B.**

**The 80 percent-as-central framing (focus Q4): not transferable, and I recommend against it.** The 80 percent stickiness number descends from 401(k) auto-enrollment (Madrian–Shea and successors): employer plans, in-paycheck employer matches, higher-wage workforces. The only at-scale test of auto-enrollment *on this proposal's population through this mechanism* is the state programs, and they delivered 48–62 percent — and that is participation, before persistence. My stress test: applying 80 percent to the universal route *with* the 0.70 persistence factor returns $14.13B — the persistence adjustment claws back essentially everything the stickiness assumption adds. $19.09B as the central case requires both 401(k)-grade stickiness and full-year contribution continuity, neither of which the operating evidence shows. Foreground $14.15B (or better, a persistence-adjusted ~$12.4B central with the ladder around it); keep 80 percent as the labeled-optimistic rung.

**The federal account (focus Q2): yes — this is the hill my evidence defends.** Worker-level portability genuinely beats the employer-keyed alternative, and my data say so directly: 38.2 percent annual turnover in covered firms, a 30 percent probability of twelve consecutive monthly contributions, separated-worker inflows collapsing to single digits. An EO-marketplace or employer-keyed rail rebuilds exactly that machine. The political cost of a Treasury-run account is real (Liu's lane), but on program mechanics D2 is the single best decision in the revision. One engineering note carried from round one: the substitution clause for 17 operating state programs still needs transition standards (fees, asset portability, anti-arbitrage), and Treasury should buy the states' nine years of registry and enforcement workflow, not rebuild it.

**The 200 percent floor (focus Q1).** The states cannot adjudicate it — every state number is match-free — so D3's rationale (the model assumes match-invariant participation and therefore cannot credit the floor) is honest. The behavioral bet is plausible at this population's margin, and the prior-year-MAGI basis (D7) materially helps salience: the rate is known at contribution time, unlike §6433's file-and-wait. But a bet this size needs its referee: **legislate the S2 evaluation with a randomized match-generosity arm (50/100/200) before national rollout.** That is my top condition.

**Tier 2.3, the six-month forfeiture bound.** State leakage is fast: 45 percent of OregonSaves' 2024 contributions flowed out the same year; 24.6 percent of CalSavers payroll-contributing accounts have taken a full withdrawal. Job-separation timing means a meaningful share of withdrawals happen within months of contribution. **Bound the within-six-months withdrawal share of matched contributions at 15 percent central (10–25 band)** — a cost offset on the order of $1.5–2B against my central, and a warning: for the highest-churn workers the effective match rate sits below the statutory schedule, which the brief should say.

**The anchor population (focus Q3) and debt financing (focus Q5), briefly — neither is my lane.** On Q3: the ⅔-median tuning is an admitted fudge that works today; my only operational note is that decennial re-anchoring creates a discrete eligibility jump every ten years, and the state wave-deadline experience says threshold dates drive lumpy behavior — plan the communication. On Q5: whatever the pay-for posture, the one financing item I will insist on is **appropriated day-one enforcement and administration funding** — CalSavers' four-year penalty lag was an infrastructure-funding failure, and a debt-financed match with an unfunded enforcement arm reproduces it at national scale.

## 4. Updated verdict

**Support with modifications — unchanged from round one, but the modification list is shorter and the support is firmer.** D2, D6, D7, and D10 adopted four of the things my evidence cares most about (portability, own-money liquidity, known-at-contribution match, self-employed rails). Conditions, in order:

1. **Legislate the S2 evaluation** with a randomized 50/100/200 match arm; the 200 percent floor is an unadjudicated bet and the design should carry its own referee.
2. **Add auto-escalation to the 3 percent default** (one point per year to 7–10 percent) and drop or recast the graduated 1/2/3 option, which works against D3's own logic.
3. **Re-center the public cost message**: quote a persistence-adjusted central near **$12.4B** (W-2 65 percent, self-employed 25 percent, persistence 0.70) with the $11.1–$13.7B band, demote 80 percent/$19.09B to a labeled-optimistic rung, and caveat the ten-year path for the year-one facilitation ramp.
