# Panel Assessment: Universal Account + Targeted Saver's Match Hybrid

**Panelist 02 — Dr. Renata Vásquez-Cole, labor economist (wealth inequality; racial and gender wealth gaps; gig, part-time, and low-wage workers)**
**Date: 2026-06-10**

I evaluate retirement policy by a single test: does it reach the workers the employer-based system was built to skip — the part-time cashier, the home-care aide, the gig driver, the secondary earner — or does it deliver another subsidy to people who already have a 401(k) and a contribution habit? The enacted Saver's Match under IRC §6433 fails that test on its own terms, because a refundable match that can only land in an account the worker must already have is a benefit conditioned on prior inclusion. My charge here is to test, with the repository's own microdata, whether this hybrid proposal actually fixes that — and where it still leaves the bottom behind.

All computations below are mine, run against `data/processed/universal_sm_hybrid/simulation_results.parquet` and `data/processed/sipp_modeled.parquet` (SIPP 2024 projected to TY2027, weighted by WPFINWGT; code in `code/`, outputs in `tables/` and `figures/`). Structural comparisons are at full participation unless noted.

## 1. Who gains first-time access: the universal-account route is poorer, more self-employed, and majority "truly excluded"

Of the 46.07 million eligible workers, 26.65 million (57.9 percent) would default into the federal universal account and 19.41 million (42.1 percent) route through an existing employer plan (`tables/table1_route_profile.csv`). The two populations are economically distinct. The universal-account route has median personal earnings of $27,397 (interquartile range $17,051–$38,750) against $36,108 for the employer-plan route, and median MAGI of $32,882 against $38,850. The universal-account route absorbs all 4.41 million eligible self-employed workers (16.5 percent of that route; zero on the employer route) and skews slightly older at the top (16.5 percent are 60 or older versus 13.9 percent on the employer route; median age 41 versus 39). Because the match schedule steepens at the bottom, the universal-account route carries an average match rate of 84.8 percentage points versus 65.2 on the employer route — the new infrastructure and the new generosity are pointed at the same people, which is exactly the design discipline §6433 lacks.

My sharper cut is the access typology among eligible workers (`tables/table2_access_typology.csv`):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Has existing DC account | 16.00 | 34.7 percent | $36,278 | $8.31 | 33.3 percent |
| No account, has workplace access | 8.12 | 17.6 percent | $33,060 | $4.52 | 18.2 percent |

The truly excluded — workers with no existing DC account and no workplace retirement access of any kind — are the single largest block of eligible workers: 19.35 million, 42.0 percent of the eligibility band, receiving 41.8 percent of full-participation match dollars ($10.4 billion of $24.9 billion). Including the 2.6 million whose access is unmeasured, nearly half (48.5 percent) of the program's dollars reach workers the current system cannot pay at all. Universe-wide, 35.7 million of the 145.34 million workers are truly excluded, and 54.2 percent of them fall inside the hybrid's eligibility band — a striking targeting ratio for a program covering 31.7 percent of the universe. Figure `figures/fig2_first_time_access_by_quintile.png` shows the truly excluded concentrated in the bottom two earnings quintiles. This is the structural population — disproportionately small-firm, part-time, and gig workers — that no information campaign reaches.

## 2. Incidence at the bottom: the access gate is doing most of the damage under current law

I built match-dollar incidence by **personal-earnings** decile of the full universe — not MAGI — because low personal earnings is where marginalized workers live: part-time workers, secondary earners, and gig workers with thin 1099 income (`tables/table3_incidence_by_earnings_decile.csv`; `figures/fig1_match_per_worker_by_earnings_decile.png`).

In the bottom earnings quintile (29.06 million workers; median personal earnings $18,803), the hybrid delivers an average of **$401 per worker per year versus $65 under current law** with its de facto DC-account gate — a factor of 6.2 (`tables/table4_bottom_quintile_summary.csv`). Decomposing that gap is instructive. Holding current-law parameters (50 percent rate, $2,000 base) but removing the access gate raises the bottom-quintile average from $65 to $187; moving to the hybrid's 200-percent-floor schedule plus the universal account takes it to $401. The gate alone, in other words, suppresses roughly two-thirds of what even the stingy current-law parameters would deliver to low earners. The microdata show why: 69.8 percent of bottom-quintile workers sit inside the current-law MAGI band — they are exactly whom §6433 targets — but only 24.4 percent of those in-band workers hold a DC account that could receive the deposit. Bottom-quintile DC ownership is 32.1 percent versus 67.6 percent across the rest of the distribution. Section 6433 as enacted is a match for the one-in-four poorest workers who already made it inside the system.

Two honest qualifications from my own tables. First, even under the hybrid, the lowest decile averages $285 against $518 in decile 2: a contribution-keyed match — even at a 200 percent rate — still scales with the capacity to contribute, and 3 percent of $11,000 is $330. The very poorest workers cannot be fully reached by any matching architecture alone. Second, only 74 percent of decile-1 workers are eligible, because eligibility is keyed to household MAGI: low-earning secondary earners in higher-MAGI households are excluded even though their individual retirement savings (disproportionately women's) are exactly where the gender wealth gap accumulates. Both caveats matter for design, and neither appears in the proposal document.

## 3. The demographic blind spot, filled with external evidence

The simulation frames carry no race, ethnicity, or sex columns, so demographic incidence cannot be computed from this repository and must be inferred. The external record makes the inference straightforward. Roughly 56 million private-sector workers — nearly half — lack access to any workplace retirement plan, and the gap is racially concentrated: 63 percent of Hispanic workers and 52 percent of Black workers lack access, well above the 47 percent national rate, and 79 percent of all unserved workers earn $53,000 or less (David John, Manita Rao, and Gary Koenig, "Payroll Deduction Retirement Programs Build Economic Security," AARP Public Policy Institute, 2024, https://www.aarp.org/content/dam/aarp/ppi/topics/work-finances-retirement/financial-security-retirement/2024-payroll-deduction-retirement-fact-sheets/payroll-deduction-retirement-programs-build-economic-security.doi.10.26419-2fppi.00164.001.pdf.). Account ownership — the current-law gate itself — is even more skewed: 61.7 percent of white non-Hispanic households held DC or IRA assets in 2022 against 34.8 percent of Black households and 27.5 percent of Hispanic households, and just 13.2 percent of households with income under $30,000 (Elizabeth A. Myers, "Ownership of Retirement Accounts in 2022: Amounts in Defined Contribution Plans and Individual Retirement Accounts," Congressional Research Service R48143, 2024, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48143/R48143.3.pdf.). On the structural margins I study: only 47 percent of part-time private-sector workers and 49 percent of workers in the lowest-wage quartile of occupations have plan access, with participation at 23 percent for both groups (John J. Topoleski, Elizabeth A. Myers, and Sylvia L. Bryan, "Worker Participation in Employer-Sponsored Pensions in 2025," Congressional Research Service IF13185, 2026, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF13185/IF13185.1.pdf.). A match gated on account ownership therefore reproduces the racial wealth gap by construction; a universal default account is the only architecture in this debate that breaks that gate. My computed result — 42 percent of hybrid match dollars flowing to workers with no account and no access — is, given these coverage patterns, very likely a downpayment on racial-gap convergence, though the repository should add SIPP's race and sex variables and publish that table rather than leave it to inference.

## 4. Political economy: why §6433 passed, and why it is stuck

The Saver's Match passed because it was bipartisan plumbing, not redistribution: SECURE 2.0 rode an omnibus (P.L. 117-328) after near-unanimous committee votes, converting the nonrefundable Saver's Credit — claimed by only 5.7 percent of taxpayers, averaging $191 (Mark J. Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.) — into a refundable deposit (CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). The coalition that could pass delivery-mechanism reform could not pass an account, a default, or new money — so the statute shipped without them. The implementation record since confirms the flaw is binding: Notice 2024-65 flagged that plans are not required to accept match deposits; no proposed regulations have issued as of June 2026; and the IRS's own advisory council warns that the program's success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions" (ASPPA, "Prioritize the Saver's Match, Advisory Council Tells IRS," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.). A first-effective-year match in 2027 with no destination account for the workers it targets is not a technical gap. It is the program.

## 5. The executive order: a fee-capped storefront is not an on-ramp

The April 30, 2026 order establishing TrumpIRA.gov does real, if narrow, work: by January 1, 2027 Treasury must list IRAs that accept §6433(e)(2)(C) match deposits, cap net expense ratios at 0.15 percent, and prohibit minimums (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.). That attacks the deposit-destination problem and fees. But it creates no account, no auto-enrollment, no default contribution, and no new dollars — saving remains opt-in through tax filing and voluntary IRA opening. The gaps I quantified are structural, not informational: the 19.35 million truly excluded eligible workers lack a payroll mechanism, not a website. Forty years of evidence — including the predecessor credit's 5.7 percent take-up and the 44 percent take-up among lowest-wage-quartile workers even when offered a plan (CRS IF13185, 2026) — says voluntary, information-led architecture does not move this population. The order's own Section 6, directing legislative recommendations for codification, concedes the point. The hybrid is the statute that Section 6 is asking for.

## 6. Against RSAA: better rate geometry at the bottom, minus one important feature

RSAA (S.1526/H.R.2696) shares the universal account and 3 percent default but keys the match to the **contribution rate** — 100 percent on the first 3 percent of income, 50 percent on the next 2, plus a 1 percent automatic federal contribution, phasing out at median income (S.1526, 119th Congress, https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1526.). For the lowest-income workers the hybrid's MAGI-keyed 200 percent floor is mostly superior: a $15,000 single filer faces a 132 percent rate and reaches the full $1,000 on a $760 contribution (5.1 percent of earnings), whereas under RSAA the maximum federal contribution for that worker is 5 percent of income — $750 — and requires the same 5 percent effort. The hybrid's flat $1,000 cap is progressive relative to RSAA's proportional-to-income cap, which scales generosity *up* with earnings. But RSAA's 1 percent automatic contribution is the one instrument in this debate that does not scale with the capacity to contribute — it reaches the worker who opts down to zero — and the hybrid folded it away. My decile-1-versus-decile-2 result ($285 versus $518) is precisely the gap a small non-contingent contribution would close. Morningstar's RSAA simulations also flag the crowd-out risk both designs share — projected wealth losses of 12 to 20 percent for younger cohorts with substantial future DC coverage (Spencer Look and Jack VanDerhei, "Will the Retirement Savings for Americans Act Save Retirement?," Morningstar, 2024, https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/will-retirement-savings-americans-act-save-retirement.) — and the repo's simulation explicitly does not model employer responses; that caveat carries over in full.

## 7. Is a 3 percent default affordable at the bottom?

Three percent of the bottom-quintile median ($18,803) is $564 a year, about $47 a month, from workers in a population where 37 percent of adults would not cover a $400 emergency expense entirely with cash or its equivalent and 18 percent could not cover even a $100 emergency from savings (Board of Governors, *Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2024*, 2025, https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/2024-report-economic-well-being-us-households-202505.pdf.). A 132–200 percent match is a powerful offset — each dollar deferred returns two to three dollars of retirement wealth — but the liquidity is locked, and an early withdrawal both triggers tax consequences and, under §6433(d)(2)'s recapture logic, claws back future match eligibility. For EITC families whose refund is the year's main liquidity event, an opt-out default that thins take-home pay can be regressive in consumption terms even when progressive in wealth terms. The design needs an emergency-savings sidecar or penalty-free small-dollar liquidity tier so that the rational response of a hand-to-mouth worker is not simply to opt out — which would concentrate opt-outs, and therefore foregone match dollars, exactly where I have shown the program's equity case lives.

## Verdict: **Support with modifications**

This is the only proposal on the table whose dollars I can trace, in microdata, to the workers the system structurally excludes: 42 percent of match dollars to people with no account and no access, and a six-fold increase in bottom-quintile per-worker subsidy over enacted law. The EO cannot do this and RSAA does it less progressively. My modifications:

1. **Add a small non-contingent federal base contribution** (on the order of RSAA's 1 percent, or a flat $150–$250 floor) for eligible workers, so the very lowest earners — for whom 3 percent of earnings is structurally small — are not left at $285 per worker while decile 2 receives $518.
2. **Pair the 3 percent default with a liquidity tier**: an emergency-savings sidecar or penalty-free withdrawal band coordinated with EITC timing, so liquidity-constrained workers stay enrolled rather than opting out and surrendering the match.
3. **Publish demographic incidence and a secondary-earner analysis.** Add SIPP race, ethnicity, and sex variables to the simulation frame and report incidence directly; and quantify the low-earnings workers excluded by household-MAGI eligibility (26 percent of decile 1), with a person-earnings eligibility test as a costed alternative — this is where the gender dimension of the design will be won or lost.
