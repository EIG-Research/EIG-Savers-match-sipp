**Panelist 02 — Dr. Renata Vásquez-Cole, labor economist (wealth inequality; racial and gender wealth gaps; gig, part-time, and low-wage workers)**
**Date: 2026-06-11 — addendum to my first-round assessment (preserved unchanged)**

All figures below are the current canonical numbers (IRS-anchored design: 44.47M eligible, ~30.6 percent of the 145.34M-worker universe; pivots $32,235 / $48,353 / $64,471; cost ladder $14.15B / $19.09B / $23.87B; average match ~$537–539). My distributional spot-checks were recomputed on the current `simulation_results.parquet` (code in `code/reassessment_checks.R`); first-round figures are cited only to mark what changed.

## 1. Disposition check

The changelog's reading of my three recommendations is accurate, and I accept all three labels.

- **Non-contingent base contribution → Deferred (D5).** Correct. It is out of the baseline, documented as a live future option, and connected to D3/D4 for joint pricing.
- **Liquidity sidecar → Partial (D6).** Correct. Own contributions are Roth-liquid with hardship and loan access; the six-month match forfeiture replaces §6433(d)(2)'s three-year recapture. No dedicated emergency sidecar.
- **Demographic + secondary-earner incidence → Open (Tier 2.6).** Correct, and I note with approval that the secondary-earner distortion is now stated plainly in the spec's marriage section rather than left to a panelist's table.

## 2. Deferrals and partials: where I concede, where I hold

**D6 (liquidity) — I largely concede.** My first-round objection was that an opt-out default thinning the take-home pay of hand-to-mouth workers would drive opt-outs precisely where the equity case lives. The RSAA withdrawal architecture answers the core of that: a worker's own money never locks, the only thing at stake on an early withdrawal is the match on that contribution, and the bite shrank from a three-year testing period to six months. The rational hand-to-mouth response is no longer "opt out" — it is "enroll, and treat the account as a costly-but-accessible buffer." That is most of what a sidecar buys, delivered inside one account. What I still want is small: the *incidence* of the six-month forfeiture is itself regressive — the workers most likely to withdraw inside six months are the liquidity-constrained bottom — so when Tier 2.3 bounds the forfeiture, it should report that bound by income band, not just in aggregate, and the legibility layer (D7) should say in plain language that withdrawing early costs only that contribution's match. EITC-season coordination (deposit timing, refund-linked top-ups) remains a worthwhile implementation note. None of this blocks support.

**D5 (base contribution) — deferral is acceptable, conditionally, and the condition has teeth.** The rationale ("defers rather than decides") is honest, and I accept that a demogrant-adjacent instrument may be a bridge too far for this draft's coalition. But the problem it solves did not defer with it. Recomputed on the current schedule, the bottom personal-earnings decile of *eligible* workers (median earnings $5,465) receives a mean full-participation match of **$202**, against **$479** in decile 2 — a contribution-keyed match, even at a 200 percent floor, scales with the capacity to contribute, and three percent of very little is very little. That dip is at least as sharp as the one I reported in round one. Deferral is acceptable to me on three conditions: (1) the "documented option" carries an actual costed line — the optional Tier 2 one-liner in D5 should be exercised, not parked; (2) if the graduated 1/2/3-percent default (D4) is ever adopted, the base contribution must be re-priced *with* it, because the graduated default cuts bottom-end contributions and therefore bottom-end match dollars — it widens the decile-1 dip by design, and adopting it without D5 would move the package backwards at the bottom; and (3) the S2 evaluation measures the dip directly, so the option's future trigger is evidence rather than vibes.

**Tier 2.6 (demographic/secondary-earner) — open, as labeled; my advice below.**

## 3. Fresh eyes

**The five focus questions.**

1. **The 200 percent floor (D3): keep it, and I will defend the override.** The model assumes participation is invariant to the match rate, so its "no new savers" result is an artifact of an assumption, not a finding — the floor's case is behavioral and concentrated among liquidity-constrained workers whose marginal dollar is hardest to move out of consumption. A $500 contribution unlocking $1,000 is the only offer in this debate sized to that population. But a bet defended as untestable-in-the-model must be tested in the world: the S2 evaluation (randomized 50/100/200 match-generosity arm) should be *legislated, with pre-specified demographic strata* — race, sex, and earnings tercile — so we learn not just whether the floor creates savers but for whom.
2. **The federal account (D2): the portability is the equity feature; pay the political cost.** Job churn, vesting schedules, and cash-out-at-separation erosion fall hardest on low-wage, high-turnover workers — disproportionately Black, Hispanic, part-time, and female. An EO-marketplace or state-rail patchwork reproduces the opt-in storefront problem I documented in round one (TrumpIRA.gov creates no account, no default, no dollars) and adds 50-state seams exactly where workers churn. Hold the line on D2.
3. **The anchor population (D1): accept, with a maintenance worry.** Anchoring to IRS all-single-filer median MAGI is the right administrative move, and the ⅔-median rule is genuinely legible. But be honest that ⅔ was *tuned* to reproduce the worker-universe coverage that a ½ anchor would have gutted (28.06M / $12.2B): the calibration compensates for scoring a worker universe against a filer-population anchor. That equivalence is not a law of nature — the filer-vs-worker median gap will drift as the filer pool ages and as gig 1099 filers enter — so each decennial re-anchor should publish a worker-median cross-check, and the cleaner long-run fix is to anchor on IRS single filers *with positive wage or self-employment income*: still administrative, but the right population. Between re-anchors, C-CPI-U indexing means eligible-share erosion lands at the band's edge — around $43,000 single — which is secondary-earner and HoH territory; the decennial re-anchor is the safeguard, and it must survive drafting.
4. **The 80 percent central framing: defensible, but publish the distribution, not just the level.** For W-2 workers under a sticky payroll default, 80 percent is reasonable and the SIPP-observed 59.0 percent is plainly conservative. My concern is compositional: a uniform 80 percent almost certainly overstates the 4.26M self-employed eligibles (no payroll, three new rails) and may overstate take-up at the bottom, where opt-outs concentrate among the liquidity-constrained. The program can hit 80 percent on average while missing the workers it exists for. So: foreground $19.09B, always with the $14.15B–$23.87B band attached, and let Tier 2.1 publish rail-specific rungs — W-2 payroll-default high, self-employed materially lower, anchored on state auto-IRA participation evidence. On Tier 2.2, the 0.59 state auto-IRA persistence factor is a sensible *lower* anchor: those programs offer no match, and a 200-percent-floor match plus D6 liquidity should hold contributors better than an unmatched auto-IRA.
5. **Debt financing (D8): acceptable for a design document; the offset is directional, no more.** I will not condition support on a pay-for, and from my seat the ten-year cost (~$216B at the 80 percent headline, FY2027–FY2036, range $191B–$228B) is modest against the $9.2B/yr current-law baseline that reaches one-third the workers at half the match. The RAND asset-test offset should stay clearly labeled directional. If Congress demands a pay-for, the distributionally coherent place to find it is inside the existing DC tax-expenditure envelope, which flows overwhelmingly to the top — but that is a fight the bill's sponsors should choose, not the spec.

**Advice on Tier 2.6 (the extension I asked for), with one honest self-correction.** Add SIPP race, ethnicity, and sex to the extract and publish match-dollar incidence by race × sex × earnings tercile; my refreshed access typology (truly excluded — no account, no workplace access — now 18.9M workers, 42.4 percent of eligibles, receiving 42.0 percent of full-participation dollars; bottom-quintile mean match $393 versus $65 under gated current law) makes racial-gap convergence very likely, but it should be shown, not inferred. On the secondary-earner margin, my own recomputation softens my first-round complaint: the 3.88M bottom-earnings-decile workers excluded by joint-MAGI keying (26.7 percent of that decile) sit in households with **median MAGI of $125,556**, 81 percent MFJ. These are low *earners* in affluent *households*. A person-earnings eligibility test would therefore dilute income targeting substantially, and I no longer push it as a costed alternative. The gender critique stands in narrower form — joint keying still ties a married woman's retirement subsidy to her household's income, and the EITC-style participation distortion is real — so Tier 2.6 should quantify the sex composition of the excluded and of the distortion-exposed band, and the right remedies if it binds are spousal-contribution mechanics, not individual MAGI.

## 4. Updated verdict

**Support** — upgraded from first-round *support with modifications*. The design moved toward me on the binding constraint (liquidity, via D6), kept the channel architecture that carries the entire equity case (D2), acknowledged the secondary-earner margin in text, and deferred — rather than rejected — the base contribution with the option door open. My conditions:

1. **Publish Tier 2.6 (demographic and secondary-earner incidence) before external release** of the brief's distributional claims; the equity case should rest on shown tables, not inference.
2. **Legislate the S2 evaluation with demographic strata and a decile-1 outcome**, so the 200-percent-floor bet and the deferred D5 base contribution are both resolved by evidence within a defined window.
3. **Package discipline on the bottom end:** if the graduated 1/2/3-percent default (D4) is adopted, the D5 base contribution must be priced and decided jointly with it — the two move bottom-decile incidence in opposite directions.
