**Date:** 2026-06-11
**Scope:** Synthesis of the ten independent persona assessments in this folder, restructured as a working document: scoreboard and findings first (§1–§4), then a tiered, checkable improvement plan (§5) and a decision log (§6). Each panelist worked from the same factual record (`_shared/policy-context.md`, `_shared/data-guide.md`) and the same model outputs, but ran their own code, downloaded their own sources, and reached their own verdict. Folder-level detail, figures, and citations live in each panelist's subfolder.

**How to use this document:** work the checklists in §5 top-down. Tier 1 items are unambiguous fixes — check them off as completed. Tier 2 items are scoped model extensions — each has a "done when" criterion. Tier 3 items are design decisions only the author can make — each is framed as a question with the panel's options; record the choice in §6. ## 2. Where the panel converges

**(a) The access/default lever does the work; the 200 percent floor mostly does not.** This is the panel's strongest cross-lens agreement, reached independently three ways:

- Lindqvist's decomposition of the $14.95B headline: access/auto-enrollment expansion $7.95B (53 percent), inframarginal transfers to existing savers $5.70B (38 percent), net new rate generosity above 50 percent only $1.29B (9 percent) (`03-lindqvist-behavioral/`).
- Mwangi's cost-per-marginal-new-saver table: default-only creates the same 17.98M new savers at $294 each that the full hybrid creates at $831 each; the 200 percent floor "buys transfers, not savers," and sits roughly 4× outside the experimental support (Duflo et al. 2006 tested matches only up to 50 percent) (`10-mwangi-program-evaluation/`).
- Hale's marginal-cost decomposition: of the $15.7B ceiling increment over current law, 79.8 percent is a rate increase for workers already eligible under §6433; incremental cost per incremental eligible worker is $1,212 vs. the $278 current-law average (`01-hale-public-finance/`).

**(b) The §6433 access gate is real, large, and the right target.** Mwangi computes that the deposit-destination gate destroys about 60 percent of current-law match offers ($9.19B in offers under universal access shrinks to $3.68B as enacted); bottom-decile mean offers are $77 as enacted vs. $630 under the hybrid. Vásquez-Cole: the bottom earnings quintile gets $401/worker under the hybrid vs. $65 under gated current law (6.2×), and 19.35M eligible workers (42 percent) have neither an account nor workplace access yet receive 41.8 percent of hybrid match dollars. Even Calloway concedes the access-gap diagnosis.

**(c) The headline take-up assumption is optimistic but inside the evidence band.** Bridger's state-calibrated central estimate is $11.4B (76 percent of the $14.9B headline survives; range $10.1–$15.3B), with persistence — not contribution levels — doing the damage. Lindqvist's rail-specific repricing band is $10.3–$19.3B. Both replicate the repo headline exactly before perturbing it, which raises confidence in the pipeline itself.

**(d) TY2027 implementation is not achievable.** Brandt: the federal account must open ~57.3M accounts (more than half never match-eligible), 13.3M defaulters are self-employed with no payroll rail, 15.6 percent of defaulters contribute under $500/yr — under the EO's 0.15 percent fee cap that is ~$0.96 of first-year revenue against ~$20/account cost — and 40.3M sub-cap eligibles need MAGI true-up at filing. Recommended phase-in: five to seven years. Bridger's CalSavers-anchored compliance ramp puts year-one cost at $9.1B for the same reason from the employer side.

**(e) The EO/TrumpIRA.gov is complementary, not sufficient.** Every panelist treats the April 2026 executive order as an information/marketplace remedy that fixes the *deposit destination* problem at near-zero cost but supplies no default and no enrollment rail. The behavioral panelists (Lindqvist, Mwangi) predict low take-up from a listing portal on the Saver's Credit precedent; the skeptics (Hale, Calloway) argue it captures most of the achievable access benefit at a tiny fraction of the cost. Liu reads its Section 6 codification ask as a genuine legislative opening.

**(f) Versus RSAA.** General agreement that MAGI-keyed targeting is distributionally tighter than RSAA's contribution-rate-keyed match, but RSAA's 5 percent default with auto-escalation is better aligned with observed state practice than the hybrid's flat 3 percent (Bridger is most emphatic; Lindqvist and Brandt concur on the default-rate point). Several panelists independently flag RSAA's 1 percent non-contingent contribution as the one RSAA instrument worth importing.

## 3. Where the panel splits

- **Median-indexed pivots.** Calloway's central objection: indexing eligibility to the median locks ~31.7 percent of workers in permanently and lets the nominal ceiling creep ($24.9B → $36.2B on his projection), where current law self-sunsets (CPI-indexed thresholds erode to ~15 percent of workers by 2051 on his calculation). Okafor wants the growth path published rather than abandoned; Raghunathan recommends replacing SIPP re-anchoring with a statutory administrative-data anchor; Liu recommends CPI-indexing the pivots as a political concession. No one defends SIPP-wave re-anchoring as drafted.
- **The federal default account.** Calloway and Liu would swap the default channel to the EO marketplace / state auto-IRAs (Liu on coalition grounds: ICI/ARA/IRI opposition plus the MyRA precedent killed RSAA's account, and 57.9 percent of eligibles routing to a Treasury account re-runs that fight). Brandt, Vásquez-Cole, Okafor, and Mwangi keep the federal account but stage it. Calloway's scale point: $87–146B annual contribution flow vs. the TSP's $44.7B.
- **The 200 percent floor.** Mwangi would stage it at 100 percent pending causal evidence; Hale would strip the rate increase entirely; Vásquez-Cole defends bottom-loading but notes the contribution-keyed design still pays decile 1 less than decile 2 ($285 vs. $518) and would add a non-contingent base contribution.

## 7. The composite recommendation implied by the panel

If one policy emerged from the ten briefs, it would: keep the universal default account but phase it in over five-plus years with the EO marketplace and state auto-IRAs as interim and substitute rails; raise the default contribution to 5 percent with auto-escalation; stage the match floor at 100 percent with a legislated pilot/RD evaluation at the eligibility frontier before going higher; replace SIPP re-anchoring with a statutory index; fix the self-employed contribution mechanism in statute; and publish the take-up sensitivity band as the headline rather than a single point. That package would likely convert both opposes into reluctant supports (each says so in their conditions) at roughly half to two-thirds of the proposed headline cost.
