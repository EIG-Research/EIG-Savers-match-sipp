## 1. The ten-year window: a $150–250 billion proposal that grows, replacing a $9 billion program that shrinks

The repository reports the proposal as an annual cost ladder — $14.9 billion headline, $19.9 billion at 80 percent participation, $24.9 billion at full participation, in tax-year 2027 dollars (`data/processed/universal_sm_hybrid/scenario_results.parquet`). A budget office does not score annual snapshots; it scores fiscal-year paths. I built one (`tables/ten_year_projection.csv`, `figures/fig1_ten_year_cost_paths.png`) under three stated assumptions. First, nominal wage growth of 3.9 percent per year, consistent with CBO's February 2026 outlook: nominal GDP growth of roughly 3.9 percent per year over FY2026–2036 (revenues of $5.6 trillion at 17.5 percent of GDP in 2026 against $8.3 trillion at 17.8 percent in 2036) with a roughly stable wage share (42.3 percent of GDP in 2026, 42.0 percent in 2027) (CBO, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036*, February 2026, https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882; House Budget Committee summary, "CBO Baseline February 2026," https://budget.house.gov/imo/media/doc/cbo_baseline_february_2026.pdf.). Second, a participation phase-in of 70/90/100 percent of steady state in TY2027/2028/2029, reflecting account provisioning and the slower self-employed channel. Third, fiscal-year timing: §6433-style matches deposit after the return is filed, so TY2027 money lands in FY2028 and FY2027 outlays are zero — the same lag visible in JCT's score of the enacted match, which shows nothing before FY2028.

The comparison line comes from the §103 row of JCX-21-22: the enacted Saver's Match was scored at −$2,097 million in FY2028 declining to −$1,687 million in FY2032, a ten-year (FY2023–32) total of −$9,318 million (JCT, *Estimated Revenue Effects of H.R. 2617*, JCX-21-22, December 22, 2022, in repo.). Two facts deserve emphasis. First, scale: the proposal's headline ten-year cost ($151.5 billion) is roughly ten times the enacted match's cost over the same FY2027–36 window (about $15.2 billion including my extrapolation). Second, and more important for scoring politics, the sign of the growth rate. JCT's §6433 profile *declines* about 5.3 percent per year in nominal terms, because the statute's MAGI thresholds are CPI-indexed off static 2027 levels while the $2,000 contribution base is not indexed at all — nominal wage growth steadily erodes eligibility and generosity. The proposal's median-anchored pivots do the opposite: they hold the eligible share of workers constant by construction, so the program grows with the wage bill, roughly 3.9 percent per year, forever. This converts a self-extinguishing tax provision into a permanent entitlement indexed to aggregate wages — a categorical change in fiscal character that the proposal document does not state plainly and that any committee staff will notice immediately. One honest caveat cuts the other way: my projection wage-indexes the entire schedule, including the $1,000 per-individual cap. The proposal text indexes the pivots but is silent on the cap; if the cap stays fixed in nominal dollars, the path bends below 3.9 percent growth — the same erosion mechanism that shrinks §6433. Cap indexation is therefore a first-order, currently unspecified, scoring parameter.

## 2. Scale: small against an upside-down $567 billion stack

Figure 2 (`figures/fig2_scale_benchmarks.png`, data in `tables/scale_benchmarks.csv`) places the proposal against the existing subsidy architecture, using JCT's FY2027 tax-expenditure estimates (JCT, *Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures for Fiscal Years 2024–2028*, JCX-48-24, December 11, 2024, https://www.jct.gov/publications/2024/jcx-48-24/.). The retirement tax-expenditure stack totals $566.5 billion in FY2027: defined contribution plans $323.1 billion, defined benefit plans $172.6 billion, IRAs $50.4 billion, and self-employed (Keogh) plans $20.4 billion. The proposal's headline is 2.6 percent of that stack; its full-participation ceiling is 4.4 percent. It is roughly one quarter of the EITC ($65.9 billion in FY2027, revenue plus outlay, same source), seven times the enacted Saver's Match in its first real year ($2.1 billion, FY2028), and infinitely larger than the executive order, which spends approximately nothing.

The distribution of the existing stack is the policy argument. CBO found that in 2019 the top income quintile received 63 percent of retirement tax expenditures ($126.7 billion of $201.6 billion) while the bottom quintile received 1 percent ($1.4 billion) (Peter G. Peterson Foundation, "Tax Breaks on Retirement Savings: Who Benefits and How Much Do They Cost?," 2025, https://www.pgpf.org/article/tax-breaks-on-retirement-savings-who-benefits-and-how-much-do-they-cost/.); the Tax Policy Center's chartbook shows benefits as a share of income peaking in the 80th–99th percentiles, with the bottom three quintiles receiving little (Toder, Khitatrakun, and Boddupalli, "Tax Incentives for Retirement Savings," Tax Policy Center, 2020, https://taxpolicycenter.org/sites/default/files/publication/159231/tax-incentives-for-retirement-savings.pdf.). Recomputing the proposal's incidence from the row-level simulation, 67.6 percent of its match dollars go to the bottom quintile of the worker universe and 95.2 percent to the bottom two quintiles; nothing reaches the top half (`tables/distribution_shift.csv`). Applying CBO's 2019 shares to the FY2027 stack and adding the proposal at the margin, the bottom-quintile share of total federal retirement subsidies rises from 1.0 percent to 2.7 percent (headline) or 3.8 percent (ceiling), while the top-quintile share falls from 63.0 to 61.4 or 60.3 percent. (Caveat: my proposal deciles are worker-universe deciles and CBO's are household quintiles, so this is an approximation, but the direction and order of magnitude are robust.) The proposal is, in short, a 2.6 percent marginal addition that is roughly 60 times better targeted than the average dollar of the existing stack. From a pure budget-allocation standpoint, that is an unusually high distributional return per dollar — the relevant question is not whether $15 billion is affordable but why it would be financed with new borrowing rather than within the $567 billion envelope.

## 3. National saving: positive, because of the default — not the match

The match is a deficit-financed transfer; national saving rises only if induced private saving exceeds the public dissaving. I computed a transparent range (`tables/national_saving_range.csv`) using Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen, "Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2014: roughly 85 percent of individuals are passive savers for whom automatic contributions pass through nearly one-for-one to total saving, while subsidies generate about one cent of new saving per dollar of expenditure among the active savers who respond to them. The headline scenario splits cleanly by route in the pipeline: $9.94 billion of match flows through the universal-account branch and $5.01 billion through the employer-plan branch, and the universal branch carries $13.98 billion of policy-induced 3 percent default contributions (full-participation contributions of $23.38 billion times the 59.8 percent take-up).

| Low | 0.75 | 0.30 | 19.5 | −14.9 | +4.6 | +$0.31 |

| High | 0.95 | 0.70 | 26.3 | −14.9 | +11.3 | +$0.76 |

Each federal dollar generates $1.31–$1.76 of new private saving, for net national saving of +$4.6 to +$11.3 billion per year — central estimate about +$8 billion, or +$0.53 per federal dollar. The decomposition matters: virtually all of the gain comes from the auto-enrollment default capturing passive savers on the universal-account route, not from the match itself. The counterfactual makes the point sharply — delivering the same $14.9 billion as a pure price subsidy with no default architecture would yield roughly *minus* $14.8 billion of national saving under Chetty et al.'s subsidy estimate. The universal account is the saving policy; the match is the participation and distribution policy. These are back-of-envelope figures: they exclude debt-service compounding on the cumulative $151.5 billion, labor-supply responses, and employer-match displacement, all unmodeled in the repository as well.

## 4. Scoring risks, the fiscal environment, the EO, and RSAA

**Scoring risks.** The repository is candid that take-up uncertainty dominates: the ladder spans $14.9–24.9 billion (67 percent), versus roughly ±2 percent indicative sampling error and a ±15 percent band on the SIPP MAGI proxy. I add a benchmark the proposal omits: JCT's enacted-match score implies far lower effective participation than this pipeline assumes. JCT's FY2028 figure ($2.1 billion) is about 23 percent of the repository's own $9.2 billion full-participation estimate for the same enacted parameters. Some of that gap is the access gap the proposal cures, but some is filing frictions, plan acceptance, and the §6433(d)(2) distribution recapture (unmodeled here, cost-inflating). A JCT score of this proposal could plausibly land below the headline in early years and above it later as auto-enrollment compounds. The self-employed channel (4.41 million eligibles with no payroll mechanism) is the single largest take-up wildcard, and the MAGI proxy moves the pivots themselves, not just the count. None of this is disqualifying; all of it belongs in the proposal's front matter.

**Pay-for politics.** The fiscal backdrop is hostile to unoffset transfers: CBO projects the FY2026 deficit at $1.9 trillion (5.8 percent of GDP), cumulative deficits of $24.4 trillion over ten years, debt held by the public rising from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 120 percent in 2036 — a record — and net interest of $16.2 trillion that exceeds defense spending every year of the window (CBO, February 2026; House Budget Committee summary, 2026.). In that environment a $151–253 billion ten-year cost will need an offset to move. The natural one is staring at us in Figure 2: a modest cap or rationalization at the top of the $323 billion DC exclusion — the Penn Wharton Budget Model has sketched exactly this swap, funding automatic accounts for low-income workers by capping the 401(k) exclusion (PWBM, "Automatic Retirement Savings Plans for Low-Income Households," July 2024, in `_shared/sources/`.). Financing the match within the retirement envelope would also neutralize the national-saving drag from borrowing and make the distributional reform explicit rather than incidental.

**The executive order.** TrumpIRA.gov costs approximately nothing and changes no parameters: it is a listing-and-suasion exercise that attacks the deposit-destination problem (fee-capped IRAs that must accept §6433 match deposits) but creates no accounts, no defaults, and no new money (policy-context.md; White House, April 30, 2026.). Does a near-zero-cost alternative lower the marginal value of spending $15 billion? No — it raises it. The EO builds acceptance infrastructure the statutory match will need anyway, while the history of opt-in delivery shows why infrastructure without defaults fails: only 5.7 percent of taxpayers claimed the predecessor Saver's Credit, averaging $191 (AEI, Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," May 5, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.). My national-saving table quantifies the same point from the other direction: the default contribution flow, which only statute can create, is what makes the program saving-positive. The EO is a complement that de-risks implementation, not a substitute that obviates spending.

**RSAA.** The Retirement Savings for Americans Act (S.1526/H.R.2696) shares the account architecture but keys its match to contribution rates up to 5 percent of pay plus a 1 percent automatic contribution, phasing out at median income. From a scoring vantage point its decisive weakness is that after three Congresses it has no JCT or CBO score at all — the only quantitative anchors are RAND's preliminary budget-model analysis (RAND, RRA2614-3, 2024, in `_shared/sources/`.) and Morningstar's simulations showing 12–20 percent *lower* projected wealth for younger cohorts via crowd-out of richer private defaults (Look and VanDerhei, 2024, in `_shared/sources/`.). An unscored percent-of-payroll entitlement is unnegotiable in committee. This proposal's transparent cost ladder, $1,000 per-individual dollar cap (which bounds per-capita exposure the way RSAA's percent-of-income match does not), and replication-ready pipeline are a genuine scoring-readiness advantage — the closest thing in this space to a JCT-submittable design.

## Verdict: Support with modifications

The proposal buys a 2.7-fold improvement in the bottom-quintile share of retirement subsidies for 2.6 percent of the existing stack, plausibly raises national saving by about $8 billion per year, and is better prepared for official scoring than any competing design. My conditions:

1. **Finance it inside the retirement tax-expenditure envelope.** Pair the match with a cap on the top end of the $323 billion DC exclusion (PWBM-style). On the current debt path — 101 to 120 percent of GDP — a deficit-financed version both invites defeat and forfeits part of its own national-saving rationale.
2. **Resolve the indexing question explicitly, and show Congress the path.** Median-anchored pivots make this a wage-bill-indexed permanent program (+3.9 percent per year) where §6433 shrinks (−5.3 percent per year); the $1,000 cap's indexation is unspecified and is first-order over ten years. Publish an FY2027–2036 outlay table (like `tables/ten_year_projection.csv`) as the headline, not a single-year cost, and consider a statutory re-anchoring review as a cost circuit-breaker.
3. **Close the scoring gaps before seeking a score.** Validate the SIPP MAGI proxy against tax data, model the §6433(d)(2) distribution recapture, and publish a dedicated self-employed take-up sensitivity — the three items most likely to move a JCT estimate away from the headline, in both directions.
