**Date: 2026-06-12 (re-review of the 2026-06-11 IRS-anchored design)**

All counts below are recomputed on the current pivots (Single $32,235 / HoH $48,353 / MFJ $64,471; 44.47M eligible) from `simulation_results.parquet` via `code/04_reassessment_checks.R` (`tables/reassessment_checks.csv`). My first-round figures were on the old pivots and are cited only to mark changes.

## 1. Disposition check

The changelog reads my three recommendations correctly. **Phase-in → out of scope (D9):** accurate as a description of what was done. **MAGI sensitivity → addressed differently (D7):** accurate, and I note with some satisfaction that the prior-year basis was the load-bearing half of my own recommendation — the brackets were the safe harbor I proposed *because* I assumed a current-year basis. **Admin subsidy → noted only (D11):** accurate. So: no quarrel with the dispositions as read; my quarrels are with two of the dispositions as decided.

## 2. Declines and scope-outs

**D7 — I concede the central point, with three specification gaps.** Keying the match to prior-year MAGI genuinely dissolves the reconciliation problem I mapped. The 40.3M-worker true-up population (now 39.08M sub-cap eligibles on the current pivots) ceases to exist as a *reconciliation* population the moment the rate is a known quantity at contribution time — there is no estimate to true up, no clawback, no ACA-APTC apparatus. This is the same lookback-certainty architecture Medicare IRMAA and income-driven student-loan repayment already run at scale, so I can certify it as buildable. Declining my brackets is also right once the basis flips: brackets existed to shrink reconciliation exposure; with none left, they would only add notches. I concede both halves. Three things the statute must still say:

1. *The determination calendar.* "Prior-year MAGI" is not known on January 1 — the prior-year return is filed in April (or October). A January–April contribution needs a rule: most-recently-filed-return basis (effectively year t−2 early in the year, IRMAA-style) is the clean answer. The spec is silent.
2. *The recurring non-filer, not just the first filer.* D7 names the no-prior-year-return edge case as new entrants. The bigger population is workers below the filing threshold — who have no prior-year return *every* year and sit exactly at the 200 percent floor. The fix is administrative, not heroic: deem workers with W-2/1099 information returns but no 1040 to the floor rate via information-return matching. The IRS already has the data.
3. *The entrant default.* I sized the entry cohort: ~2.29M eligible workers aged 18–21 (1.46M on the federal route) as a lower-bound proxy, ~6.02M through age 24. My recommendation between D7's two candidate rules: default to the most-generous band pending the first filed return. The floor population is genuinely low-income, the cap is $1,000, and a current-year-estimate rule reintroduces a miniature reconciliation for the one population least equipped to handle it.

**D9 — I hold, narrowly.** A steady-state design document is a legitimate artifact, and the explicit scope statement prevents the implied-TY2027 misreading I feared. But the scope-out does hide the binding constraint: the constraint on this program is not the schedule, it is the build — my first-round five-to-seven-year estimate (TSP modernization, GAO-24-106319; CalSavers' three-year employer waves) stands, and nothing in the revision touches it. Worse, the design is not fully separable from the calendar: D1 anchors to "the IRS median for the year before implementation," yet SOI publishes filing-status tables with a roughly two-year lag — the repo's own anchoring work uses TY2023 data in 2026 and bridges with a ×1.126 projection. A statute drafted from this document would be unadministrable as written. The fix costs one sentence each: define the anchor as the *most recent published* SOI median (with the projection convention stated), and acknowledge in the scope statement that stand-up is multi-year and the effective date is a load-bearing parameter for the anchor year. I narrow my objection to that.

**D11 — my objection stands in full.** The spec's treatment is the "few basis points of assets" sentence plus "immaterial for the headline transfer cost." True for the transfer line; the ratio framing is exactly the fallacy my arithmetic was built to expose. Recomputed on the current design: the federal-route median default contribution is still $1,282/year; 15.6 percent of the 57.32M defaulters contribute under $500 (6.7 percent under $250; 37.7 percent under $1,000); at the EO marketplace's 0.15 percent fee cap the median account yields **~$0.96 of first-year revenue against a ~$20 recordkeeping cost**, with fee-breakeven at a ~$13,300 balance — and 31.34M of those accounts belong to workers who will never receive a match dollar. This is not a transition matter that D9 can absorb: with CalSavers averaging ~$2,544 per funded account after seven years, the deficit *is* the steady state for the first decade-plus. At $20–30 per account-year the unbudgeted line is roughly $1.1–1.7B/yr — 8–12 percent of the $14.15B headline. A design document that prices the match to the dollar while carrying a ~10 percent unfunded adder as one sentence is understating its own cost. Either appropriate the subsidy or state explicitly that the fee cap will be breached. This remains my top condition.

## 3. Fresh eyes

**The five focus questions.** (1) *200 percent floor:* from my chair, the floor is the cheapest part of the machine to run — a parameter, not a process — and since the model assumes match-invariant participation it cannot adjudicate the bet. Keep it, but legislate the S2 evaluation *with* the program; randomized generosity arms cannot be retrofitted onto a live entitlement. (2) *Federal account vs. marketplace:* I side with D2, on the record. The EO marketplace creates listings, not accounts, defaults, or money; worker-level portability is the genuine fix for churn and cash-out erosion. But the unpriced D11 economics and the unacknowledged build timeline are precisely the two things that make the marketplace-plus-state-rails alternative look cheaper than it is — defending D2 honestly requires conceding both. And the substitution clause still lacks the glide path I asked for: a literal TSP-like fee test disqualifies every existing state program (their stacks run 25–100+ bp plus flat charges). Write the ten-year fee step-down or the states become casualties, not on-ramps. (3) *Filer-vs-worker anchor:* the IRS anchor is a clear administrative win — published, replicable, SIPP-free — and ⅔-of-median is honest calibration, legibly drafted. My caveat is the publication-lag point above, plus a note that the all-single-filer median moves with demography (retirees, student filers), not just worker wages, so decennial re-anchors can jump discontinuously. (4) *80 percent as central:* defensible for the W-2 payroll rails, where auto-enrollment evidence supports it. It is not defensible for the 13.26M self-employed defaulters, for whom "default" still means opt-in on every rail D10 names. With 4.26M self-employed eligibles (~9.6 percent of eligibles), the framing error is bounded — but the brief should say "80 percent on payroll rails, materially lower on the self-employed rail," which is what Tier 2.1/2.13 will eventually quantify. (5) *Debt financing:* mostly Okafor's lane; my one institutional note is that the RAND offset runs through *delayed SSI/Medicaid eligibility*, which presumes balances count against asset tests — a treatment advocates will fight to reverse. Cite it as directional color, never as a pay-for.

**New administrative surface.** *D6 (six-month vesting):* a substantial upgrade — it deletes the cross-plan, three-year 1099-R lookback I called the hardest systems-build in the enacted statute and replaces it with six months of match-to-contribution lot tracing, which is routine sub-accounting. Two specifications needed: a withdrawal-ordering convention (Roth ordering treats contributions as a pool; the statute must say whether a withdrawal is deemed to come from oldest contributions first — FIFO protects savers and I recommend it), and a forfeiture-reporting feed from employer-plan recordkeepers to Treasury. On Tier 2.3, my advice: bound the forfeiture share at low single digits of matched dollars — leakage is concentrated at job separation, not inside six months of contribution. *D10 (self-employed rails):* operability ranking — platform-facilitated (with the classification safe harbor, which is correctly identified as the legally load-bearing element) > estimated-tax piggyback (buildable on EFTPS/Direct Pay, but aimed at the worst-compliance filers) > 1099-payer withholding (no voluntary 1099-withholding infrastructure exists outside backup withholding; small payers will resist a new obligation). For Tier 2.13 I would defend nothing above the teens in year one for this rail.

## 4. Verdict

**Support with modifications — unchanged in label, materially narrowed in content.** D7 dissolved my biggest objection; D6 deleted the second-hardest build; D10 is a real rail design. The verdict moved toward support. Conditions: (1) price the small-dollar administrative subsidy or openly abandon the 0.15 percent fee standard (D11 — unchanged from round one); (2) specify the D7 determination calendar, the recurring-non-filer floor rule, and the most-generous-band entrant default; (3) define the anchor off the most recent *published* SOI median and write the state-program fee glide path into the substitution clause.
