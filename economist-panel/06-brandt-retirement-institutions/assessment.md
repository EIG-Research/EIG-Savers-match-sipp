# Can Anyone Actually Run This? An Implementation Assessment of the Universal Account / Saver's Match Hybrid

**Panelist 06 — Dr. Eleanor Brandt, retirement institutions and plan administration**
**Date: 2026-06-11**

I evaluate retirement policy from the machine-room floor: ERISA plan administration, recordkeeping economics, payroll integration, and the IRS systems that have to move the money. My question for this proposal is not whether the match schedule is well targeted — my distributional colleagues will adjudicate that — but whether Treasury, the IRS, recordkeepers, payroll providers, employers, and the state auto-IRA programs can deliver it, on what timeline, and what breaks first. My benchmarks are the institutions that have actually done versions of this job: the Thrift Savings Plan, CalSavers, OregonSaves, and Illinois Secure Choice.

## The political economy the proposal inherits

The enacted Saver's Match (SECURE 2.0 §103, IRC §6433) passed because it was the rare redistributive retirement provision with genuine bipartisan lineage — the Cardin–Portman conversion of the nonrefundable Saver's Credit into a refundable, Treasury-paid deposit, folded into an omnibus that cleared the Senate 68–29 (CRS, "The Retirement Savings Contribution Credit and the Saver's Match," IF11159, 2025, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11159.). But Congress legislated a payment without building a payee. The match must land in an account; nothing in §6433 creates one, and nothing compels any plan or IRA to accept the deposit. Treasury's own implementation record concedes this: Notice 2024-65 spends most of its 30 questions on deposit destinations, erroneous payments, the recovery tax, and how Treasury "completes" a contribution when no account exists (IRS, "Notice 2024-65," 2024, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-65.pdf.). Eighteen months later there are still no proposed regulations, and the IRS Advisory Council's January 2026 message was blunt: the program's success "depends upon convincing retirement plan and IRA administrators to accept the contributions" (ASPPA, "Prioritize the Saver's Match, Advisory Council Tells IRS," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/1/prioritize-the-savers-match-advisory-council-tells-irs/.). The proposal under review is best understood as the statutory fix for exactly this flaw — and it should be judged by whether its fix is buildable.

## The administrative load map

I sized the populations each institution must handle, using the repo's simulation frame (`code/01_admin_load_map.R`; `tables/admin_load_map.csv`; `figures/fig1_admin_load_map.png`). Auto-enrollment covers the whole 145.34 million-worker universe, not just the 46.07 million match-eligibles — a point the proposal's cost tables understate because cost follows the match while workload follows the universe.

| Institution / rail | Total workers (M) | Match-eligible (M) | Not eligible (M) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal universal account — W-2 workers (payroll rail exists) | 44.06 | 22.24 | 21.81 |
| Federal universal account — self-employed (no payroll rail) | 13.26 | 4.41 | 8.85 |
| Employer plans (must accept federal match deposits) | 88.03 | 19.41 | 68.61 |

Three loads stand out. First, the federal account must open and service **57.3 million accounts**, of which 30.7 million belong to workers who will never receive a match dollar — they are pure cost centers for the recordkeeper. Second, **13.3 million defaulters are self-employed** (4.41 million of them match-eligible, confirming the spec), and for them the word "default" is aspirational: there is no employer, no payroll system, and no withholding to default. The technical spec concedes the contribution mechanism is unspecified (Glasner, "Universal Account + Targeted Saver's Match Expansion," §12.5, 2026.). Quarterly estimated-tax piggybacking is the only realistic rail, and Schedule SE filers are precisely the population with the most volatile income and the weakest filing compliance. Third, the 88 million workers routed to employer plans imply that essentially **every 401(k), 403(b), and governmental 457(b) recordkeeper in America must build §6433 deposit-acceptance machinery** — aggregation files, sub-accounting for match dollars (which carry their own hardship-withdrawal exclusions and recovery-tax tracing under §6433(f)), Form 5500 and 5498 reporting changes — that Notice 2024-65 questions 11–29 show does not yet exist even on paper. Current law makes acceptance voluntary; this proposal must either mandate it (an ERISA preemption and cost fight the industry has already signaled it will wage) or accept that match dollars for plan participants default into the federal account, doubling account fragmentation.

## Small-dollar accounts: the economics nobody headlines

The proposal says administrative cost is "immaterial for the headline transfer cost." For the transfer line, yes. For viability, no. From `tables/small_dollar_economics.csv` and `figures/fig2_small_dollar_economics.png`: the weighted median default contribution on the universal-account route is **$1,282 per year**; **15.6 percent of defaulters generate less than $500 per year** (6.7 percent under $250; 37.7 percent under $1,000).

Set that against real per-account costs. The Center for Retirement Research's auto-IRA cost model, anchored to California, Illinois, and Oregon program experience and administrator conversations, puts third-party-administrator recordkeeping at roughly **$20 per account per year**, with program fees of about $24 flat plus 25–35 basis points (Aubry, "A Massachusetts Auto-IRA Program," Center for Retirement Research at Boston College, 2024, https://crr.bc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/A-Massachusetts-Auto-IRA-Program.pdf.). The TSP — the proposal's explicit benchmark — spent $502 million gross ($429 million net) on administration in 2023, a 4.8 basis-point net ratio that pencils out to roughly **$61–72 per participant per year**, and is cheap in ratio terms only because the average TSP balance exceeds $100,000 (FRTIB, "TSP Annual Review: Administrative Expenses for 2023," 2024, https://www.frtib.gov/pdf/minutes/2024/Jan/Att5-2023-TSP-Annual-Review-of-Administrative-Expenses.pdf.). If the universal account honors TSP-like fees — the executive order's marketplace analog caps net expenses at 0.15 percent — then the median new account generates **about $0.96 of first-year fee revenue against a ~$20 cost**; 99.4 percent of accounts run a first-year deficit, and fees do not cover cost until a balance reaches roughly **$13,300**. CalSavers' average funded account holds $2,544 after nearly seven years (CalSavers, "About the Program," statistics as of 3/31/2026, https://www.calsavers.com/home/about.html.). The arithmetic conclusion: Congress must appropriate an explicit recordkeeping subsidy — on the order of $20–30 per account, $1.2–1.7 billion per year across 57 million accounts, roughly 8–11 percent of the $14.9 billion headline transfer — for at least the program's first decade, or the fee cap is fiction. The proposal budgets zero for this.

## Match-delivery mechanics: a year-round payroll program keyed to an April number

The match is `min(rate(MAGI) × contribution, $1,000)` per individual. Contributions flow every pay period; the rate is a linear function of MAGI that nobody — not the worker, not the recordkeeper, not Treasury — knows until the return is filed. Under current law's flat 50 percent rate, MAGI matters only at the phaseout cliffs. This proposal makes the rate MAGI-sensitive *everywhere*: every dollar of income revision moves the match for every sub-cap worker. From `code/02_reconciliation_map.R` (`tables/reconciliation_process_map.csv`; `figures/fig3_reconciliation_exposure.png`):

| Population | Workers (M) | Process required |
|---|---|---|
| All match-eligibles (rate strictly between 0 and 200) | 46.04 | Year-end MAGI determination; match deposited "as soon as practicable" after filing — a structural one-year lag behind contributions |
| Sub-cap eligibles (match < $1,000) | 40.28 | Full true-up: median **$32 of match moves per $1,000 of MAGI revision** (p75 $47, p90 $55) |
| Cap-binding eligibles (match = $1,000) | 5.79 | Locally insensitive to MAGI news — the simplest cohort |
| Within $2,000 of the zero-match endpoint | 2.48 | Cliff-adjacent: a bonus or a spouse's overtime zeroes the match after contributions were made |
| Self-employed eligibles, universal account | 4.41 | Neither contribution nor MAGI observable until filing |
| All eligibles: §6433(d)(2) distribution recapture | 46.07 | Three-year lookback netting prior distributions against qualified contributions — unmodeled in the simulation, and it requires cross-plan 1099-R matching *before* any match is paid |

Because the match is computed at filing, "clawback" in the payroll sense is avoidable — but the cost is that the subsidy arrives 12 to 16 months after the saving behavior it is meant to reinforce, which is precisely the timing failure that made the old Saver's Credit invisible (claimed by roughly 5.7 percent of taxpayers, average $191; Warshawsky, "TrumpIRA.gov," AEI, 2026, https://www.aei.org/economics/trumpira-gov/.). Any attempt to advance the match into the year — which advocates will demand — imports the ACA advance-premium-credit reconciliation apparatus, including repayment caps, for 40 million people. And the §6433(d)(2) lookback plus the §6433(f)(6) recovery tax already constitute, in IRSAC's view, the hardest systems-build in the enacted statute; this proposal triples the eligible population subject to them while the IRS has yet to issue a single proposed regulation.

## State auto-IRAs: partners or roadkill

The SIPP extract carries no state geography, so I benchmark externally (`tables/state_auto_ira_benchmarks.csv`). As of early 2026, all fifteen active state auto-IRA programs combined hold a bit over **1 million funded accounts and $2.5 billion** (Pew, "Status of State 'Auto-IRA' Savings Programs," 2026, https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/02/04/status-of-state-auto-ira-savings-programs.). CalSavers, the largest, reached 629,000 funded accounts and $1.6 billion in roughly seven years (CalSavers, 2026.); OregonSaves holds about 180,000 accounts and $430 million after eight and a half years, with withdrawals on some 40 percent of accounts (OPB, "OregonSaves has $430M in assets, but people keep taking money out," 2025, https://www.opb.org/article/2025/12/23/oregon-saves-oregonsaves-retirement/.); Illinois Secure Choice holds about 170,000 savers and $339 million and is spending mid-2026 executing a recordkeeper conversion (ASPPA, "Illinois Secure Choice: New Tech, Name in Relaunch," 2026, https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/5/illinois-secure-choice-new-tech-name-in-relaunch/.). The proposal's universal-account route is **57 times the entire state system and 91 times CalSavers**. The substitution clause (employers may use state or private auto-IRAs meeting "TSP-like" standards) is the right harmonization instinct, but as drafted it is a preemption trap: no state program today operates anywhere near a 0.15 percent all-in fee — their fee stacks run 25 to 100+ basis points plus flat dollar charges, because their average balances are $2,000–$3,000 — so a literal TSP-like fee standard would disqualify every existing program and strand nine years of state institution-building. Conversely, grandfathering them at current fees creates a two-tier system where the workers the states already enrolled pay 5–6 times the federal fee. The proposal needs an explicit glide path: deem state programs compliant for, say, ten years contingent on fee step-downs, and let the federal account serve as their back-end at marginal cost.

## The EO, the timeline, and RSAA

The April 2026 executive order and TrumpIRA.gov attack the deposit-destination problem from the only side an EO can: a Treasury-vetted marketplace of IRAs that volunteer to accept §6433(e)(2)(C) deposits, capped at 0.15 percent fees with no minimums (White House, "Promoting Retirement-Savings Access for American Workers by Establishing TrumpIRA.gov," 2026, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/04/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpira-gov/.). It creates no accounts, no defaults, and no money — but it does create the listing infrastructure and the §104 awareness machinery, and its Section 6 invites codification. The correct reading, which the proposal's authors should embrace rather than dismiss: the EO marketplace is the *interim deposit rail* the universal account needs for its first years.

Because Treasury cannot stand up a TSP-like platform by TY2027. The evidence is not speculative. The TSP's own recordkeeping modernization — a replacement system for an *existing* program with 7 million participants and $895 billion — took from a 2020 contract award to a June 2022 go-live that GAO found was launched without verified testing or milestone completion, fielding 120,000 calls on day one with wait times reaching two hours, on a contract valued near $4.6 billion (GAO, "Thrift Savings Plan: Investment Board Needs to Greatly Improve Acquisition Management and Contractor Oversight," GAO-24-106319, 2024, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106319.). CalSavers needed three years of phased employer-size waves just to complete registration deadlines, and seven to reach 629,000 accounts; Illinois needed months for a routine recordkeeper swap. A federal account onboarding 57 million workers — plus an IRS match engine handling 46 million MAGI reconciliations and a three-year distribution lookback — is realistically a **five-to-seven-year build with cohort phase-in**, even with clean appropriations. First match deposits under the enacted statute are already expected in 2028 for TY2027 (Mercer, "Treasury and IRS start warming up for SECURE 2.0's saver's match," 2024, https://www.mercer.com/insights/law-and-policy/treasury-and-irs-start-warming-up-for-secure-2-0-s-savers-match/.); this proposal should not promise the same year for a vastly larger machine.

On employers: the auto-enrollment mandate reaches firms the state programs deliberately exempted. Pew's employer surveys found about 80 percent of OregonSaves employers reported no out-of-pocket cost, but onboarding ran slow precisely among the smallest firms, and states enforce with per-employee penalties of $100–$500 (Aubry, 2024.). A federal mandate on sub-ten-employee firms without a payroll-provider integration standard and a multi-year size phase-in will replicate CalSavers' compliance lag at fifty times the scale.

Against RSAA (S.1526), the comparison is instructive: RSAA keys its match to the worker's *contribution rate* — observable in real time on the payroll file — and phases out on income only at the credit-limit margin. That is administratively cleaner per worker than this proposal's MAGI-keyed rate, which makes every match a tax-season computation. RSAA also skips the employer-plan mandate entirely (only uncovered workers enter). The hybrid buys sharper bottom-targeting (200 percent floor, $1,000 cap) at the price of importing EITC-style reconciliation into a payroll program. That trade may be worth it — but only if the reconciliation is engineered deliberately, not inherited by accident.

## Verdict: Support with modifications

The diagnosis is right, the account architecture is right, and the proposal fixes the genuine flaw — the access gap — that is currently stalling §6433. But as specified it is not implementable on its implied timeline or budget. My three modifications:

1. **Legislate the phase-in, not just the program.** W-2 workers at firms with 25+ employees first (leveraging existing payroll rails and the EO marketplace/state programs as interim deposit destinations); sub-25 firms in years two to four; self-employed last, via an estimated-tax contribution election. Set the first universal-account match year no earlier than three full years after enactment, and fund the IRS §6433 systems build (the (d)(2) lookback, the recovery tax, and the deposit files) as a named appropriation.
2. **Blunt the MAGI sensitivity.** Replace the continuous linear schedule with three to five flat rate brackets on *prior-year* MAGI as a safe harbor, with a de minimis no-recovery band (no adjustment for match errors under ~$100, mirroring the $32-per-$1,000 median exposure I compute) and ACA-style caps on any reconciliation liability. This preserves the distributional shape while cutting the 40.3 million-person true-up to a bracket-boundary population.
3. **Price the small-dollar problem honestly.** Appropriate a flat per-account administrative subsidy (roughly $20–30 per account-year, about $1.2–1.7 billion) declining as balances cross the ~$13,000 fee-breakeven threshold, and write the state-program substitution standard as a ten-year fee glide path rather than an immediate TSP-equivalence test, so CalSavers, OregonSaves, and Illinois become on-ramps instead of casualties.

With those amendments, this is the most credible deposit-destination fix yet put on paper — more honest than the EO's voluntary marketplace and better targeted than RSAA. Without them, it is a beautiful schedule attached to a machine that does not exist.
