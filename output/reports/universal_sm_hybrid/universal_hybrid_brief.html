A Universal Saver’s Match

Design, reach, generosity, and cost of a redesigned federal retirement-savings match

June 9, 2026

A universal, automatic retirement account paired with a redesigned federal match would extend a meaningful savings subsidy to roughly 46.1 million low- and moderate-income workers at an estimated headline cost of about $14.9 billion per year.

Why it matters. The savings match enacted in federal law rewards only workers who already hold a workplace retirement account and who contribute enough to claim it. Workers without a plan at work — independent contractors, gig workers, and employees of firms that offer nothing — are left out, and the lowest earners gain the least. The design described here pairs the match with a savings account that every worker can use, raises the subsidy rate sharply at the bottom of the income scale, and lowers the amount a worker must set aside to receive the full credit.

What the policy does

The proposal has two moving parts. First, every covered worker is automatically enrolled — at a default contribution of three percent of earnings — into a retirement account. Workers already covered by a qualifying workplace plan keep that plan; everyone else is routed into a new universal account modeled on a low-cost, portable federal design. This closes the access gap that keeps the current match from reaching the workers it was meant to help.

Second, the federal match itself is redesigned. Rather than a flat 50 percent match that stops at a fixed income cutoff, the match rate becomes a single declining line that depends on a worker’s income and tax-filing status. The rate begins at 200 percent for the lowest earners — three federal dollars for every one the worker saves becomes one dollar of credit for every fifty cents saved — and falls steadily as income rises, reaching zero at the top of each eligibility band. The federal credit is capped at $1,000 per individual, so a married couple in which both spouses work can receive up to $2,000 between them.

Because the schedule is anchored to the income distribution rather than to fixed dollar thresholds, the eligibility band scales automatically with filing status and can be re-anchored as incomes change over time.

Where the eligibility band falls

Each filing status has a pivot — the income level at which the match rate has declined to 50 percent. Below the pivot the rate is higher (rising to 200 percent at zero income); above it the rate keeps falling along the same line until it reaches zero, where the match ends. Because the pivot is set from the income distribution rather than a fixed dollar cutoff, it scales with filing status and can be re-anchored over time.

Who it reaches

The estimates rest on a large, nationally representative U.S. household survey that records each person’s earnings, income, household composition, and access to a workplace retirement plan. Survey weights are applied throughout so that every figure reflects the national population rather than the sample count.

The analysis begins with the roughly 259.9 million U.S. residents age 18 or older and narrows to the working population most relevant to the policy: people with earnings and a determinable tax-filing status, after setting aside dependents and full-time students. That leaves a universe of about 145.3 million workers. Within it, an estimated 46.1 million workers — close to 32 percent of the universe — fall inside the redesigned eligibility band and would qualify for a positive match.

Eligible workers reach the match through one of two channels. Those who already hold a workplace plan claim it there; the rest reach it through the new universal account. The universal account carries the larger share of eligible workers, both because it absorbs self-employed workers and because workers without a plan at work tend to have lower incomes — and therefore higher match rates.

How generous it is

The redesign is most visible at the bottom of the income scale, where the current match does the least. Three measures make the point, using a single filer as the example.

  • A far higher subsidy rate. At very low earnings the match rate is 200 percent, meaning a worker who sets aside just $500 receives the full $1,000 federal credit. The rate is still 100 percent at about $21,919 of income, where a $1,000 contribution — roughly 4.6 percent of earnings — captures the full credit.
  • An easier path to the maximum credit. Under current law a worker must contribute the full $2,000 to receive the $1,000 maximum. Under the redesign, low earners reach the same maximum on a fraction of that amount, which matters because lower-income workers were never likely to set aside $2,000.
  • A larger average award. Across all participants the redesign delivers an average federal match of about $543 per year, set against roughly $278 per worker under the current-law match at full participation in the same population. The two averages rest on different participation assumptions, so read this as a broad comparison rather than a like-for-like one.

The federal dollars are concentrated where the need is greatest. Of the full-participation match, an estimated 83 percent flows to the lowest-earning 30 percent of the workforce (the bottom three income deciles), and nearly all of it to the lower-earning half. The highest-income workers receive nothing, because the match phases out entirely before their income range begins.

What it costs

The headline estimate is an annual federal cost of about $14.9 billion, reaching 27.5 million participating savers at a take-up rate of about 60 percent. Cost depends heavily on how many eligible workers actually participate, which is why the estimate is presented as a range rather than a single number.

Participation scenario Take-up Participants Annual cost
Headline (observed participation behavior) 60 percent 27.5 million $14.9 billion
Automatic enrollment at 80 percent participation 80 percent 36.9 million $19.9 billion
Full participation (upper bound) 100 percent 46.1 million $24.9 billion

Two features of the cost range stand out. The structural ceiling — the cost if every eligible worker participated fully — is about $24.9 billion, so the program cannot cost more than that at any plausible behavioral response. And the headline sits well below that ceiling because participation, while high under automatic enrollment, is not universal — the policy’s reach depends on the automatic account doing its job.

How the estimates were produced, and what to keep in mind

The cost and reach figures come from a microsimulation: the policy rules are applied to each worker in the survey, the federal match is computed for that worker given their income, filing status, and savings channel, and the results are summed using national population weights. Incomes are projected forward to the policy’s first effective tax year using a standard wage-growth assumption so that the dollar thresholds line up with the year the policy would take effect.

A few assumptions shape the numbers and should travel with them. The estimates are descriptive: they show what the policy rules would cost and whom they would reach under stated participation assumptions, not a behavioral forecast of how saving or work decisions might change. Participation is the largest source of uncertainty, which is why the cost is reported as a range; and the federal match is paid only on contributions workers actually make, so the estimates assume the automatic three percent default is contributed. Because the underlying data are a sample of households, the figures are point estimates rather than exact counts; they are not reported with statistical confidence intervals, so small differences should not be over-interpreted. The income measure is drawn from a household survey and approximates — but does not exactly equal — the income concept a tax agency would use, so the eligibility thresholds would shift modestly under a tax-administered version. The survey’s incomes are aged forward to the policy’s first year for dollar comparability, but the population’s composition and saving behavior are not re-projected. The estimates also leave aside administrative costs, the cost of running the universal account, and any rules governing early withdrawals; each matters for an implementing statute but not for the headline transfer cost reported here.
